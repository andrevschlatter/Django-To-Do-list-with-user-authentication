# Nvidia rations the credit behind AI's build-out

*As of 22 November 2026. This report gives no buy or sell recommendation. It sets out the facts and the strongest version of each side.*

Nvidia became "the bank of the AI ecosystem" without lending money: it holds about $114bn of equity stakes, many of them in its own customers, has promised to buy up to $32bn of capacity that neoclouds cannot sell, and has guaranteed up to $118bn of customers' obligations, $105bn of it for one OpenAI campus in Ohio. None of the guarantees has cost it a dollar, and all of it is small beside $44bn of quarterly free cash flow, which is the core of the bull case. The bear case is that this support was what made the weakest buyers financeable, and that it is now being rationed: interim CEO Colette Kress has said commitments "could be reduced or eliminated" if projects stall, lenders have reportedly cut loan-to-value on GPU loans from 80% to 50-60%, and the $500bn financing alliance has deployed nothing. The much-quoted $530bn of "guarantees" is a sum of three tables in Nvidia's own filing, and more than half of it is Nvidia's purchase orders to suppliers. An SEC inquiry is reported by three outlets on unnamed sources; nobody outside the SEC and Nvidia knows its focus, no wrongdoing has been alleged, and Nvidia's two previous SEC settlements, over reporting, disclosure and controls, carried no intent-based fraud charge against the company. The 10-Q is not late (it is due 4 December), and in 22 of the last 42 periods Nvidia filed its report after the earnings release. Oracle's claim to be "fully funded" for OpenAI rests on a $20bn cap on market raises plus unquantified vendor financing, customer prepayments and $288bn of landlord-financed leases; the "$30bn still to raise" the reader recalls was not an Oracle figure. Several of the background leads were wrong or imprecise, and they are corrected in the table below.

## 1. Mechanics: Nvidia sells the chips at full price, then lends its credit rating to the buyer

The phrase "bank of the AI ecosystem" is loose. Nvidia makes no loans. It does four things that have the economic effect of a bank's credit: it buys equity in customers, it promises to buy or floor the price of the capacity its customers build, it guarantees customers' leases to their landlords, and it has tried to organise outside lenders into a $500bn pipeline. Each tool sits in a different place in Nvidia's accounts and carries a different risk, and the public debate keeps going wrong because people add them together.

### Tool one: equity stakes in customers and suppliers

Nvidia buys shares in the companies that buy its chips. Its equity investments stood at **$99bn at 26 July 2026**, up from about $7bn a year earlier, with a further $25bn of equity commitments pending [filing] ([Nvidia 10-Q for the quarter ended 26 Jul 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001045810/000104581026000075/nvda-20260726.htm); [CNBC, 4 Sep 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/09/04/nvidia-ai-investments-99-billion.html), pre-exit, [search summary]). By 25 October 2026 the unaudited Q3 exhibits show $65.5bn of non-marketable (private) securities and $44.2bn of marketable equity, with $58.1bn spent on equity securities in nine months and $20bn of equity commitments still outstanding [filing] ([Q3 FY27 CFO commentary, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27cfocommentary.htm); [supplemental exhibit, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/supplementalfinancialinfor.htm)). Adding $4.7bn of locked-up public shares carried in other assets gives an equity book of about $114bn at Q3; that sum is this report's own arithmetic, because Nvidia has not yet published a Q3 equivalent of its "$99 billion" sentence.

The named positions that matter: $30bn in OpenAI's roughly $110bn round announced 27 February 2026, which replaced a September 2025 framework of "up to $100bn" that the WSJ reported had stalled [anonymous for the stall; on record for Huang's remark that the $100bn "is probably not in the cards"] ([Bloomberg, 31 Jan 2026](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-31/nvidia-pauses-plan-to-invest-100-billion-in-openai-wsj-says); [CNBC, 19 Feb 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/19/nvidia-is-in-talks-to-invest-up-to-30-billion-in-openai-source-says.html), both pre-exit, [search summary]); a pledge to buy about $10bn of shares in a private placement alongside Anthropic's IPO ([Bloomberg, 12 Nov 2026](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-12/nvidia-s-10-billion-anthropic-ipo-pledge-in-focus-with-ceo-exit), [search summary]); $2bn each in CoreWeave and Nebius in early 2026; and $5bn in Intel ([Investing.com analysis](https://www.investing.com/analysis/a-deep-dive-into-nvidias-latest-portfolio-moves-200680639), pre-exit, [search summary]).

An equity cheque does two jobs. It gives the customer cash that partly returns to Nvidia as chip orders (the "circular" complaint), and it gives lenders to that customer a signal that Nvidia stands behind it. Nvidia's risk is the ordinary risk of an investor: mark-downs. Q3 brought the first sizeable one, a **$1.8bn impairment** on private holdings, against cumulative unrealised gains of $9.9bn [filing] ([supplemental exhibit, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/supplementalfinancialinfor.htm)).

### Tool two: rent-back and capacity purchase commitments

Here Nvidia promises to be the customer of last resort for the computing capacity its chips create. There are two generations of this tool.

The first is a straightforward purchase commitment. Under a September 2025 order form, Nvidia must buy CoreWeave's unsold capacity through April 2032, with an initial value of $6.3bn, conditional on delivery and service standards [filing, via press] ([CNBC, 15 Sep 2025](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/09/15/coreweave-stock-jumps-on-disclosure-of-6point3-billion-order-from-nvidia.html), pre-exit, [search summary]). A similar arrangement with Lambda, about $1.5bn across 18,000 GPUs, was reported by The Information [anonymous] ([Tom's Hardware, Sep 2025](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/nvidia-signs-usd1-5-billion-deal-with-cloud-startup-lambda-to-rent-back-its-own-ai-chips-18-000-gpus-will-be-leased-over-4-years-as-lambda-gears-up-for-its-ipo), pre-exit, [search summary]).

The second generation, introduced in 2026, is a revenue floor with a profit share. Nvidia's filings call these "AI cloud agreements" (Q2) and now "neocloud agreements" (Q3); the filings say Nvidia "will earn revenue on the upfront sale of our infrastructure" and, if criteria are met, share in the neocloud's revenue from third-party customers. They do not use the words "take-or-pay" or "backstop" [filing] ([Q2 FY27 CFO commentary, 26 Aug 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001045810/000104581026000073/q2fy27cfocommentary.htm); [Q3 FY27 CFO commentary, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27cfocommentary.htm)). SemiAnalysis, in a mid-2026 piece this research could not open, describes the economics with an illustrative example: a GB300 GPU rents for $6.75 an hour; Nvidia guarantees the neocloud a floor of $3.68; above the floor Nvidia takes 40% of the excess ($1.23) and the neocloud keeps $1.84, so the neocloud earns $5.52 instead of $6.75 in exchange for a floor that a lender will lend against [estimate; illustrative, not deal terms] ([SemiAnalysis, "Nvidia GPU Debt Backstop Unleashes the AI Project Trinity", date not confirmed, about July 2026](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/nvidia-gpu-debt-backstop-unleashes), pre-exit, [search summary]). One disclosed deal is consistent: Sharon AI's filing describes a six-year collaboration for up to 40,000 GB300 GPUs with "a revenue-sharing and credit-support model" [filing] ([Sharon AI 8-K exhibit](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0002068385/000149315226028370/ex99-1.htm)).

The point of the floor is the lender, not the neocloud. A lender to a start-up cloud wants an investment-grade name promising the cash flow that services the loan; SemiAnalysis says lenders "require an offtake contract or a backstop from an investment grade hyperscaler" (same source, [search summary]). Note what the floor protects against. On the illustrative numbers the year-one floor is about 55% of the market rent and the six-year average floor ($2.36) is about 35% of it, so the lender is covered against rents halving, not against a modest fall (this report's arithmetic on the illustrative example).

These agreements totalled **$36bn at July 2026 and $32bn at October 2026**, and the Q3 exhibit added a sentence: "Our commitments take effect only if the neoclouds secure financing to purchase the data center infrastructure products and the related compute capacity is deployed, made operational, and accepted by us" [filing] ([Q3 FY27 CFO commentary, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27cfocommentary.htm)). Separately, Nvidia rents cloud capacity for its own research under "multi-year cloud service agreements" ($28bn at Q3); those are purchases for its own use, although critics count them too.

### Tool three: lease and credit guarantees

The third tool is a guarantee to a landlord. A data center developer will not sign a 20-year lease with a tenant that has no credit rating unless someone creditworthy stands behind the rent. Nvidia has become that someone.

The first appeared in the October 2025 10-Q: a guarantee of an unnamed partner's facility lease with maximum exposure of $860m, for which the partner placed $470m in escrow and Nvidia received warrants [filing] ([Nvidia 10-Q, Oct 2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581025000230/nvda-20251026.htm)). The category then grew by two orders of magnitude in one step. On 17 August 2026 Nvidia filed an 8-K disclosing "residual value guaranties" to SB Energy for about 4.25 GW of leases at the Portsmouth site in Pike County, Ohio, where the tenant is an OpenAI affiliate. The terms, read directly from the filing [filing] ([Nvidia 8-K, 17 Aug 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000069/nvda-20260817.htm), pre-exit): Nvidia's "aggregate payment obligation is cumulatively capped at $105 billion"; it pays only if OpenAI becomes insolvent or stops paying rent, and then generally pays the shortfall between a guaranteed minimum lease value and what the landlord recovers by reletting or selling; each guarantee takes effect only when the relevant lease commences and the landlord has met ready-for-service conditions (expected from 2028); the guarantee ends after 20 years, or earlier if OpenAI terminates the lease or "achiev[es] a satisfactory credit rating"; OpenAI has agreed to reimburse and indemnify Nvidia for anything it pays. No guarantee fee is disclosed.

At 25 October 2026 the guarantee table read **$118.0bn of maximum gross exposure: $105.0bn SB Energy, $9.5bn of new "power and systems credit support", and $3.5bn of land, power and shell guarantees for neoclouds**, with the statement "To date, we have not made any payments related to our guarantees" [filing] ([Q3 FY27 CFO commentary, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27cfocommentary.htm)). What the $9.5bn line covers and for whom is unknown; the 10-Q that would explain it has not been filed.

The sequencing is the feature to understand. On the 17 November call Kress said, as quoted by TechCrunch, that a guarantee becomes effective only after the customer has secured its own equity and debt, built the facility and installed the Nvidia equipment, and that "if all of that begins and unfortunately they are delayed or they have a challenge in terms of building that, our guarantee doesn't exist anymore" [on record; spoken quote not checked against a transcript] ([TechCrunch, 18 Nov 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/18/nvidia-clarifies-when-it-will-and-wont-keep-financing-neoclouds/), [search summary]). Nvidia therefore carries tenant-credit and residual-value risk after completion, and no construction or financing risk. That protects Nvidia. It is also what makes the guarantee less useful to the lender who has to fund the construction.

### Tool four: the $500bn financing alliance

On 10 August 2026 Nvidia announced partnerships with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR "to establish AI compute infrastructure financing platforms to mobilize over $500 billion of third-party capital" [on record] ([GlobeNewswire, 10 Aug 2026](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/08/10/3342187/0/en/nvidia-partners-with-apollo-blackrock-blackstone-brookfield-goldman-sachs-and-kkr-to-establish-ai-compute-infrastructure-financing-platforms-to-mobilize-over-500-billion-of-third-p.html), pre-exit, [search summary]). Nvidia's own description is that the figure is capital the platforms are designed to raise over time, "not Nvidia revenue, a single fund, or a commitment to a single customer", and that everything is subject to definitive agreements ([Nvidia blog](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-ai-factory-compute/), [search summary]). The idea is that the six firms would lend against GPUs and data centers, and Nvidia would make the collateral safer by offering "limited residual-value support": Huang said up to 25% on some projects ([Fortune, 12 Aug 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/08/12/nvidia-private-capital-deal-circular-financing-ai-boom/), pre-exit, [search summary]). A residual-value guarantee means that if the borrower defaults and the chips sell for less than the debt outstanding, Nvidia makes up part of the gap.

The Wall Street Journal reported after the 17 November call that the plan "has yet to put any money to work", that the August documents were non-binding memoranda of understanding obliging no firm to raise money, that lenders want more default protection than 25% (the Journal's comparison was Broadcom reportedly guaranteeing about 85% of a $35bn Anthropic financing), and that the first deal is expected in 2027 at the earliest [anonymous; WSJ not opened, relayed by other outlets] ([Investing.com, Nov 2026](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/wall-sts-500-bln-pledge-to-nvidia-yet-to-be-mobilized-wsj-reports-5037693); [Fortune, 19 Nov 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/11/19/nvidia-funding-deal-anthropic-ipo/); [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/nvidia-500-billion-financing-plan-144134035.html), all [search summary]). The filings are consistent with that: the Q3 guarantee table has no residual-value line for the platforms [filing] ([Q3 FY27 CFO commentary, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27cfocommentary.htm)). The alliance is, so far, an intention.

### A worked example: 120 MW, and what a loan-to-value cut does to the equity cheque

No single disclosed deal gives cost, leverage, rate, tenor and offtake together, so this example is a composite built from sourced ranges. Every figure marked "arithmetic" is this report's own calculation.

**The project.** A neocloud wants 120 MW of IT capacity. Industry benchmarks put the building, power and cooling at roughly $19m per MW and the IT hardware at $29m to $42m per MW; a Crusoe example cited by secondary sources is about $60m per MW, of which $40m is IT [estimate; secondary blogs citing JLL and Cushman & Wakefield] ([GR1D](https://gr1d.us/insights/data-center-cost-per-mw); [Gainam](https://gainam.com/insights/ai-data-center-cost-per-mw), dates not confirmed, [search summary]). At those rates the shell costs about $2.3bn and the hardware about $4.8bn (arithmetic). Using the Ohio campus ratio of 1.5 million GPUs across 4.25 GW, 120 MW holds about 42,350 GPUs (arithmetic; chip generations differ, so order of magnitude only).

**Who puts up what, and when.**

| Step | Party | What it provides | Where Nvidia's support sits |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Site | Developer/landlord | Builds the powered shell (about $2.3bn) with its own equity and construction debt, against a long lease to the neocloud | Lease or land/power/shell guarantee to the landlord, effective only once the phase is complete |
| 2. Customer | End customer (a lab or enterprise) | Signs a take-or-pay contract; may prepay (15% and 30% down payments appear in the sourced examples) | None directly; Nvidia may hold equity in the customer |
| 3. Equity | Neocloud | Puts equity into a special-purpose vehicle that will own the GPUs | Nvidia may hold equity in the neocloud |
| 4. Debt | Private credit funds/banks | Lend to the vehicle against the GPUs and the contract, typically a three-year fully amortising loan | Revenue floor ("neocloud agreement"), effective only once financing is secured and the capacity is deployed, operational and accepted |
| 5. Hardware | Nvidia | Ships about $4.8bn of systems and books revenue on the sale | Nvidia is paid at this point |
| 6. Operation | Neocloud | Rents capacity; cash flow services the loan | Floor pays if rents fall below it; Nvidia shares revenue above it |

Sources for the structure: three-year amortising loans repaid from take-or-pay contracts ([Longbridge summary of Odd Lots, Nov 2026](https://longbridge.com/en/news/306363613), [search summary]); sequence of conditions ([Q3 FY27 CFO commentary, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27cfocommentary.htm), [filing]).

**The loan-to-value cut.** Loan-to-value (LTV) is the loan as a share of the hardware's cost; the rest is the equity cheque.

| | LTV 80% | LTV 55% | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hardware cost | $4.80bn | $4.80bn | |
| Debt | $3.84bn | $2.64bn | −$1.20bn |
| Equity cheque | $0.96bn | $2.16bn | +$1.20bn, 2.25 times |
| Principal due per year (3-year amortising) | $1.28bn | $0.88bn | |

All arithmetic. The same $2.16bn of equity that funds one 120 MW cluster at 55% would have funded 2.25 clusters at 80%. Put the other way, a neocloud with a fixed pool of equity buys 56% fewer GPUs (1 − 0.20/0.45). That is the channel from credit conditions to Nvidia's order book.

**Cash flow against debt service.** At full utilisation 42,350 GPUs earn about $2.5bn a year at $6.75 an hour, about $2.05bn at the $5.52 net-of-revenue-share rate, and about $1.37bn at the $3.68 floor (arithmetic on the SemiAnalysis illustration). Principal alone on the 80% loan is $1.28bn a year. At the floor price the project barely covers principal before interest, power and staff, which is why the level of the floor and the length of the customer contract decide whether a lender will lend.

**How firm is the 80%-to-55% premise?** It rests on one person. Stanley Lee of Liquid Compute said on Bloomberg's Odd Lots (listed as 19 November 2026) that LTVs were "as high as 80%" in the summer, about 70% after Labor Day, and are now often 50% to 60%, with rates on 50% loans in the "mid-to-high teens"; his own worked example was a $100m cluster where equity rises from about $30m to as much as $50m, which is a 70%-to-50% move [on record, single market participant, no lender confirmation; episode not opened] ([Longbridge](https://longbridge.com/en/news/306363613); [BigGo Finance](https://finance.biggo.com/news/e930df83b00077bf), Nov 2026, [search summary]). There is documentary support for the direction: CoreWeave's March 2026 facility advanced 90% of cost during installation, and a later facility advanced 70% [secondary reading of exhibits] ([Cape Fear Advisors](https://capefearadvisors.substack.com/p/coreweaves-call-option-on-used-gpus), date not confirmed, [search summary]). Treat 80% as the summer peak and 55% as the midpoint of today's reported range.

### Why $530bn and $118bn are $412bn apart

Three reasons, in order of size. **Scope**: $279bn of the $530bn is what Nvidia has promised to buy from its suppliers, and another $87bn is its own cloud rental, leases, capex and unfunded equity. None of that is a guarantee of anyone's debt. **Definition**: Kress's figure counts only legal guarantees, so it leaves out the $36bn of neocloud revenue floors and the $20bn of third-party leases, which are customer support in economic substance but purchase and lease commitments in legal form. **Timing**: $530bn is July; $118bn is October.

Both extremes mislead. Calling $530bn "guarantees" overstates credit exposure by a factor of about five. Quoting only $118bn understates customer support, and ignores that on the broad measure total obligations rose by $137bn in the quarter while the narrow guarantee figure rose by $9.5bn. The supply commitments deserve attention for a different reason: they are the bet that demand arrives.

A defensible middle measure of **support to customers** at 25 October 2026 is $150bn (guarantees plus neocloud agreements), or $170bn with unfunded equity commitments, plus an unknown part of the $74bn lease line. Add the equity already invested, about $114bn (marketable equity $44.2bn + locked-up public equity in other assets $4.7bn + non-marketable securities $65.5bn; arithmetic on the [Q3 supplemental exhibit](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/supplementalfinancialinfor.htm)), and Nvidia's total capital at risk in its ecosystem is roughly $265bn to $285bn, of which $105bn cannot become effective before fiscal 2029. One outside number does not fit any of this: Reuters and a blog citing Bank of America put total guarantees at "about $230 billion" as of August ([Reuters via Kitco, 16 Nov 2026](https://www.kitco.com/news/off-the-wire/2026-11-16/nvidias-ai-financing-machine-may-survive-huang-less-risk-appetite), pre-Q3, [search summary]). It may include deals then under negotiation. It is unreconciled with the $108.5bn in the filing and is shown here as a conflict, not a fact.

None of these figures is the amount of customer debt that Nvidia's support makes possible. Nvidia discloses its own maximum payment. A 25% residual-value guarantee, for instance, supports four dollars of project cost for each dollar of Nvidia exposure.

### How much revenue depends on financed or guaranteed customers?

Nvidia does not disclose this. The only sized outside views found are Morgan Stanley's Joseph Moore, reported as putting neoclouds at "close to 25% of revenues… but with more coming from established players", and Bloomberg Intelligence's Vasu Kasibhotla, reported as saying neoclouds plus the rest of their reporting segment make up "nearly half" [estimate] ([TIKR via Yahoo Finance, Nov 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/neoclouds-nearly-half-nvidia-own-172124788.html), [search summary]). Neither is a measure of revenue that depends on Nvidia's financing. What follows is a range built from filed numbers, with the assumptions stated. It is this report's construction and should be read as a bound, not a measurement.

The filed inputs are these. Data center revenue in Q3 was $107.6bn, split between Hyperscale ($60.1bn, up 71% year on year) and "AI Clouds, Industrial & Enterprise" ($47.5bn, up 195%). That second segment, which this report abbreviates ACIE, holds the neoclouds and frontier labs alongside sovereign, enterprise and industrial buyers; it was **41% of total revenue** [filing] ([Q3 FY27 CFO commentary, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27cfocommentary.htm)). Nvidia also says that "one AI research and deployment company contributed a meaningful amount of our revenue by purchasing cloud services from our customers", which is almost certainly OpenAI buying through hyperscalers, so some financed demand sits inside the Hyperscale line [filing; the identification is inference] ([Q2 FY27 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000075/nvda-20260726.htm)).

### CoreWeave: the largest, the most leveraged, and a refinancing story in 2027

CoreWeave's quarter was strong on the top line and thin underneath: revenue of $3,661m, operating income of $207m, net interest expense of $846m and a net loss of $709m; it guides fourth-quarter interest expense to $1.05-1.15bn. Liquidity was $9.8bn ($4.1bn cash plus $5.7bn of undrawn facilities) against principal of $2.4bn due in the rest of 2026 and **$11.0bn due in 2027** (10-Q). Set against roughly $4.4bn a year of interest at the fourth-quarter rate, the model works only while new term loans, prepayments and vendor financing keep arriving (this report's arithmetic).

The pricing of its facilities through 2026 shows the market tightening before November: SOFR + 2.25% in March for a loan backed by investment-grade contracts, SOFR + 4.50% in May, SOFR + 5.50% in August for the first facility backed by shorter contracts (10-Q). Two disclosures deserve more attention than they have had. First, "$1.9 billion of the deferred revenue balance… was reclassified from deferred revenue to customer liabilities… as contracts became cancellable during the quarter"; the filing does not say whose. Second, its top two customers were about 30% and 24% of revenue.

The reader's lead that CoreWeave "sold about $4.2bn of converts and set up an ATM on November 10" is **partly wrong on the date**. Both were September transactions: $4.2bn of 2.875% convertible notes due 2033 were issued in September 2026, and the at-the-market programme for up to 35 million shares was signed on 17 September. They were listed as quarterly highlights in the 10 November earnings release, which is the source of the confusion. No shares had been sold under the programme by 30 September (10-Q). Convertible notes and an at-the-market programme (an "ATM", which lets a company drip new shares into the market) are equity-linked funding, and both lose their usefulness when the share price falls. At about $39.51 on 20 November, down from a 52-week high of $138.25 [unverified aggregator data] ([GuruFocus, 20 Nov 2026](https://www.gurufocus.com/news/9213331/coreweave-crwv-shares-plunge-1215-amid-tech-selloff-hits-new-52week-low), [search summary]), the stock is far below the conversion prices of all three converts ($97.85 to $119.60), so $10.8bn of convertibles are debt in practice, and 35 million shares would raise about $1.4bn against about $2.9bn when the programme was set up (arithmetic).

On credit: CoreWeave's unsecured notes were yielding about 14% to 16% in mid-October to mid-November, and its five-year credit default swaps were quoted above 1,000 basis points in mid-October [unverified data sites] ([Bond Terminal](https://bondterminal.com/issuers/coreweave-inc); [TradingNews](https://www.tradingnews.com/news/coreweave-sinks-to-60-usd-as-cds-spread-top-1000bp-and-35b-usd-debt), pre-Q3, [search summary]). Ratings as last listed in June 2026 were Ba3/B+/BB-. No quote dated after 11 November and no rating action in October or November was found. Nvidia's ties: it bought about 23 million shares for $2.0bn in January 2026, and "all of the GPUs used in our infrastructure today are NVIDIA GPUs" (10-Q).

### Nebius: capex at three and a half times revenue, funded by customers

Nebius plans $40-45bn of 2027 capex against $12-13bn of 2027 revenue. The reader's lead on prepayments is confirmed verbatim: "We expect $10 billion in committed customer prepayments in 2026, rising to $17-20 billion in 2027… The 2027 range is not a ceiling" (shareholder letter). The arithmetic of the plan: 75-85% of $40-45bn is $30-38bn; with $17-20bn from prepayments, asset-backed financing has to supply roughly $10-21bn, and the uncovered 15-25% is **$6-11bn** to be found from cash, corporate debt, equity or the ATM (this report's arithmetic). A prepayment is customer credit: it brings cash forward and creates a delivery obligation, and deferred revenue is already $9.4bn. Two things to watch: a $3.5bn Citi facility signed on 22 October has "a 364-day maturity", so it needs refinancing by about October 2027 ([Nebius release, 22 Oct 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1513845/000110465926117978/tm2628078d1_ex99-1.htm)); and deliveries are slipping, with 2026 revenue guided to the low end and New Jersey tranches for Microsoft pushed to the second quarter of 2027. Nvidia beneficially owns 9.3% ([Schedule 13G, 20 Jul 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000062/primary_doc.xml)).

### IREN: the one management-quantified funding gap

IREN's disclosure is the cleanest: of $15bn of remaining capex for its fiscal year to June 2027, $9bn is covered and **$6bn is "target" funding "subject to market conditions"**. Recent contracts show customers standing in for lenders: one five-year deal has "90% of the GPU capex prepaid by the customer".

### Nscale: the dependency chain in one prospectus

Nscale has $105.8bn of contracted value against $307.5m of nine-month revenue. Nvidia is a holder of more than 5%, a customer under a $1.2bn agreement, a participant in $2.36bn of pre-IPO convertible notes, and the guarantor of up to $860.3m of Nscale's Ward County, Texas lease, against which Nscale holds $470.0m in escrow as Nvidia's recourse. That last detail shows how these guarantees are built: more than half of the exposure is cash-collateralised by the neocloud itself, so Nvidia is lending its rating more than its money. The $44.6bn Anthropic contract requires Nscale to "use best efforts to obtain qualifying financing… within a specified period". Microsoft terminated a $3.22bn statement of work after a UK power connection was withdrawn. The going-concern discussion says management could "defer, reduce or cancel capital expenditure if such financing is not obtained" (S-1/A). The Telegraph is reported as saying Nscale is weighing a delay of its listing to 2027 [anonymous] ([MarketScreener](https://www.marketscreener.com/news/nscale-considers-postponing-35bn-ipo-telegraph-ce785edbd88df32d), pre-Q3, [search summary]).

**Lambda** supplied the clearest filed example of a contract failing for want of a building. ### Who holds Nvidia's $32bn?

Nobody outside Nvidia knows. The named pieces are CoreWeave's $6.3bn 2025 agreement, SharonAI, Firmus and Nscale; most of the $32bn, all of the $9.5bn power and systems line, and all but $860m of the $3.5bn of lease guarantees are unattributed. The 10-Q may say more.

## 4. If the markets close: growth capex stops first, Nvidia's floors lapse by their own terms, and the lenders are left with three-year-old chips

This section traces what follows if equity and debt markets shut to the neoclouds. Each link is documented as a contractual condition or a risk disclosure. With the exceptions noted, the links are not events that have happened. No filing or report found shows a neocloud default, a missed payment, a covenant waiver, a triggered Nvidia guarantee or a cancelled Nvidia order as of 22 November 2026. The ordering of the chain is this report's inference from the filed numbers.

### Link 1: uncommitted growth capex stops

The first thing to go is spending that has no committed funding behind it. The listed neoclouds have told investors how much that is: IREN's $6bn "subject to market conditions"; Nebius's uncovered $6-11bn, plus its reliance on $17-20bn of prepayments arriving; Nscale's "reliance on uncommitted debt and equity financing" and its stated remedy to "defer, reduce or cancel capital expenditure"; Core Scientific's statement that completing its build-out "is expected to require additional external financing" (sources in section 3). Three smaller issuers disclose the same gap in different words: signed customer contracts, GPUs to buy, project debt not yet arranged (Nscale, SharonAI, ChronoScale).

The equity side of this link is already partly shut. Firmus pulled its IPO in October; Nscale is reported to be weighing a delay; CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN shares were reported down 74%, 63% and 79% from their highs as of 18 November [unverified] ([Motley Fool, 18 Nov 2026](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/11/18/iren-coreweave-and-nebius-stock-prices-have-plunged-more-than-60-here-s-what-it-means-for-ai-investors/), [search summary]). At those prices convertibles do not convert and at-the-market programmes raise half as much.

### Link 2: projects stall, and stalled projects void contracts

Customer contracts in this sector are conditional on delivery. When a site is late the customer can walk. This has happened: Microsoft terminated a $3.22bn Nscale statement of work after a power connection was withdrawn [filing] ([Nscale S-1/A, 12 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2110365/000119312526458722/ck0002110365-20261112.htm)); Lambda's missed lease deadline has left a $34.9bn Anthropic agreement terminable by either party [filing] ([Anthropic 424B4, 19 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026074944/anthropicpbc-424b4.htm)); Cerebras began accruing service credits to OpenAI for late capacity on 1 October [filing] ([Cerebras 10-Q, 12 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2021728/000162828026073494/cbrs-20260930.htm)); CoreWeave reclassified $1.9bn because "contracts became cancellable" (section 3). The causes disclosed so far are power, design changes and suppliers, not money. Financing stress makes the same failure more likely, because sites and chips that cannot be funded on time are not delivered on time.

### Link 3: Nvidia's support lapses exactly then

Nvidia's neocloud commitments "take effect only if the neoclouds secure financing… and the related compute capacity is deployed, made operational, and accepted by us"; if not, they "could be reduced or eliminated" [filing] ([Q3 FY27 CFO commentary, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27cfocommentary.htm)). So the revenue floor that was meant to make a GPU loan financeable is not available to the lender deciding whether to fund the project, and disappears if the project slips. The lease guarantees work the same way, phase by phase on completion. For Nvidia this is a protection: in a closed-market scenario its $32bn of neocloud commitments shrinks, and no cash goes out. The cost shows up elsewhere.

The Ohio guarantee behaves differently. A delay postpones it. And SB Energy's own prospectus adds a fact that bears on OpenAI: for the remaining 3.78 GW of the campus, "If NVIDIA does not provide such guaranties by a date certain, OpenAI is required to secure a replacement guarantor" rated at least A- [filing] ([SB Energy S-1/A, 19 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2133037/000162828026075026/sbenergy-sx1a3.htm)). If Kress declines the option, OpenAI needs another investment-grade name.

### Link 4: the effect on Nvidia's revenue, inventory and equity book

**Revenue.** The exposed revenue is the financing-dependent part of the "AI Clouds, Industrial & Enterprise" segment, $47.5bn in Q3. Morgan Stanley's Joseph Moore is reported as putting neoclouds at "close to 25% of revenues… but with more coming from established players", and Bloomberg Intelligence as saying neoclouds plus the rest of that segment are "nearly half" [estimate] ([TIKR via Yahoo Finance, Nov 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/neoclouds-nearly-half-nvidia-own-172124788.html), [search summary]). On Moore's share, neocloud revenue is about $29bn a quarter (this report's arithmetic: 25% of $115.8bn), so each 10% cut in neocloud purchases is roughly $2.9bn a quarter, or 2.5% of revenue. The same coverage notes that consensus fiscal 2028 revenue of $689bn "assumes these customers stay funded". No analyst estimate of revenue at risk in dollars was found. The telecom precedent in section 6 is the tail case: revenue down 57% to 62% in two years when the financed customers failed. Nvidia's customer mix is far stronger than Lucent's, so that is a boundary, not a forecast.

**Inventory and purchase commitments.** Nvidia has $390bn of supply commitments and $39.7bn of inventory sized for continued growth. Supply agreements "may be cancelable, rescheduled, or adjustable… prior to placing firm orders. Changes to these agreements may result in additional costs" [filing] ([Q2 FY27 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000075/nvda-20260726.htm)). A demand shortfall therefore reaches the income statement through inventory provisions and cancellation charges before it reaches the guarantee table. Nvidia has been here before on a smaller scale: section 11 covers the inventory charges of fiscal 2023.

**Equity book.** Nvidia holds about $114bn of equity, a large part of it in customers. The first $1.8bn impairment on private holdings came in Q3. Its listed neocloud stakes have fallen with the shares: CoreWeave was carried at $4.1bn on 47.2 million shares at 30 September ([Nvidia 13F, 16 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000084/information_table.xml)); at $39.51 the same shares are worth about $1.9bn (arithmetic).

**Receivables.** Nvidia's $81bn of receivables, part of it on terms of up to a year, is concentrated in a handful of names; the extended terms go to customers Nvidia describes as investment-grade. If that description is right this link holds.

### Link 5: the lenders and landlords

Who is on the other side? Filings name the arrangers and holders: MUFG, Morgan Stanley and JPMorgan as agents on CoreWeave's term loans; Citi for Nebius; Deutsche Bank for Galaxy; Macquarie for Nscale and Applied Digital; Magnetar; US private placement investors for IREN; Dell Financial Services as vendor lender to CoreWeave and Nscale (section 3 sources). Their protection differs sharply by structure. Loans backed by an investment-grade customer's contract (Microsoft, Meta) are money-good if the customer pays. Loans against a neocloud's own credit or a private lab's contract depend on GPU resale values, and those loans are three-year amortising structures against collateral whose rental value is partly a function of credit conditions. Stanley Lee's point on Odd Lots was that borrowers shut out of term loans rent instead, pushing spot rents up; if he is right, today's high rents overstate what the collateral would fetch in a market where many clusters are for sale at once [on record, single source] ([KuCoin news, Nov 2026](https://www.kucoin.com/news/flash/tightened-financing-drives-up-gpu-rental-prices-in-ai-compute-market), [search summary]).

Signs of strain on the lender side are real but modest: ING put about $500m of data center loans up for sale with bids due 19 November (outcome not reported); a $488m Malaysian data center syndication was held up; the largest listed credit funds of Ares, Blackstone and Blue Owl reported making fewer loans in the third quarter than a year earlier [anonymous/relayed] ([CNBC, 6 Nov 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/06/ing-ai-data-center-loans-debt.html); [Bloomberg, 16 Nov 2026](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-16/asian-data-center-loan-syndication-is-delayed-in-rare-deal-pause); [Bloomberg, 12 Nov 2026](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-12/shrinking-bdcs-keep-private-credit-on-sidelines-as-rates-rise), all pre-Q3, [search summary]). Landlords with one tenant are the most exposed after the neoclouds themselves: CoreWeave has $47.4bn of leases signed and not yet commenced.

### How far along is the chain?

| Link | Status at 22 Nov 2026 | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Equity window | Largely shut for weaker names | Firmus IPO pulled; Nscale reportedly weighing delay; shares down 60-80% from highs |
| Debt terms | Tighter, tiered | LTV 80% → 50-60% (single source); CoreWeave unsecured at 14-16% and off the table |
| Delivery failures voiding contracts | Happening | Lambda/Anthropic; Microsoft/Nscale; CoreWeave's $1.9bn reclassification |

| Defaults, waivers, lender losses | Not reported | Targeted search found none |
| Order push-outs at Nvidia or server makers | Not reported | None found |

The counter-evidence is substantial, and all of it postdates Nvidia's results. Anthropic's IPO priced at $83.00 a share and began trading on 19 November, raising $81.34bn with a concurrent $26.0bn private placement in which Nvidia is a buyer [filing] (Anthropic 424B4, dated 19 November). Apollo and Goldman Sachs priced about $44bn of notes on 20 November for a vehicle that buys Nvidia chips and leases them to SpaceX, at 7.66% to 8.36%, rated A3/A-, "oversubscribed and upsized" [anonymous in part] ([Reuters via Investing.com, 20 Nov 2026](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/apollo-goldman-lead-44-billion-spacex-gpu-debt-financing-5042346), [search summary]). ChronoScale closed an equity placement on 18 November [filing] ([ChronoScale 8-K, 18 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1549084/000149315226052998/form8-k.htm)). Filed pricing for small GPU loans in October and November is about 10% fixed (SharonAI 9.95%; a Macquarie 90-day loan at 10%), well below the "mid-to-high teens" in the Odd Lots account; the two may describe different borrowers.

The pattern is tiering, not closure. Capital is available in very large size to the model developers and to structures with a strong lessee. It is scarce for the intermediaries whose credit depended on a guarantor. Even the strongest GPU-lease credit paid about 8.3% for seven-year money, which sets a floor far above the SOFR + 2.25% that contract-backed neocloud loans paid in March. A full closure would start the chain above in earnest; what the evidence shows today is the first two links engaged and the rest waiting.

## 5. The bull case: a contingent, unpaid promise that is small next to $44bn of quarterly free cash flow

This section argues the case as its best defenders would. It is advocacy by design; the counter-arguments are in section 6.

**The bank is small relative to the business behind it.** In the quarter to 25 October 2026 Nvidia reported revenue of **$115.8bn (up 103% year on year)**, data center revenue of $107.6bn, GAAP net income of $65.3bn, operating cash flow of $48.3bn and free cash flow of $43.8bn; nine-month free cash flow was $113.7bn; it guided the January quarter to $130.0bn plus or minus 3% with no China data center compute assumed [filing; unaudited 8-K exhibits] ([Q3 FY27 press release, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27pr.htm); [CFO commentary, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27cfocommentary.htm)). Maximum guarantee exposure of $118.0bn is about 2.7 quarters of free cash flow at the current rate, or roughly the last nine months of it (this report's arithmetic). And $118bn is a ceiling on gross exposure across 20-year leases, not an expected loss.

**Nothing has been paid, and the structures are built so that little will be.** Nvidia states that it has "not made any payments related to our guarantees" (same CFO commentary). The largest item, $105bn for the Ohio campus, pays only if OpenAI is insolvent or stops paying rent; even then Nvidia pays only the shortfall after the landlord relets or sells; OpenAI must reimburse Nvidia; and the guarantee ends when OpenAI earns a satisfactory credit rating [filing] ([Nvidia 8-K, 17 Aug 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000069/nvda-20260817.htm)). No obligation can begin before the first building is ready, expected in fiscal 2029; Moody's is reported as expecting obligations to begin in 2028 and peak in 2031 [estimate] ([FourWeekMBA on Moody's, Nov 2026](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-moodys-cuts-nvidia-outlook-on-ceo-exit-keeps-aa1-rating/), [search summary]). Chris Wood, whose September 2026 essay is the fullest written defence, points out that about two-thirds of what critics call "circular financing" is this one conditional guarantee, and concludes: "Nvidia is not a bigger Lucent. Lucent's balance sheet and cash flows could not justify financing customers. Nvidia's can." [opinion; essay not opened] ([Grow or Die, about 10-14 Sep 2026](https://growordieinvesting.substack.com/p/why-nvidias-circular-financing-does), pre-exit, [search summary]). His description of the Ohio terms matches the 8-K. One of his supporting numbers is now stale: he cited $250m of cumulative write-downs on the private portfolio, and the Q3 exhibits show $2.2bn of cumulative losses and impairments.

**The neocloud exposure, the part people worry about, is a rounding error.** Neocloud agreements are $32bn, down from $36bn, and neocloud land, power and shell guarantees are $3.5bn (same CFO commentary). $32bn is 6.9% of annualised Q3 revenue (arithmetic: $115.8bn × 4 = $463bn). And these commitments switch on only after the neocloud has raised its own money and the capacity is running, so Nvidia is not exposed to the projects most likely to fail.

**Most of the revenue comes from the best credits in the world.** Hyperscalers were $60.1bn of the $107.6bn of data center revenue in Q3, or 56% [filing] ([CFO commentary, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27cfocommentary.htm)). Nvidia's November 2025 memo to analysts said that "unlike Lucent, NVIDIA does not rely on vendor financing arrangements to grow revenue" and that customers pay in about 53 days [on record] ([Yahoo Finance, Nov 2025](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/nvidia-says-it-isnt-using-circular-financing-schemes-2-famous-short-sellers-disagree-100021210.html), pre-exit, [search summary]). Days sales outstanding has since risen to 64, and Nvidia attributes that to Rubin shipment timing and "extended payment terms on large, multi-quarter agreements with certain investment-grade customers" (CFO commentary). A bull reads that as accommodating the strongest buyers, not propping up the weakest.

**End demand is real and accelerating.** Anthropic's amended S-1 reportedly shows run-rate revenue of about $81bn at 31 October 2026, up from about $9bn at the end of 2025 (run-rate here is trailing-28-day consumption times 13; one analysis annualises actual quarterly revenue to about $68bn) [filing via secondary] ([24/7 Wall St, 11 Nov 2026](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/11/11/anthropic-says-its-revenue-run-rate-jumped-to-an-81-billion-run-rate/), pre-exit by a day, [search summary]). CoreWeave's backlog was $132.5bn at 30 September with revenue up 168% ([Motley Fool transcript, 10 Nov 2026](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/11/10/coreweave-crwv-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/), pre-exit, [search summary]). Rental prices for B200 GPUs rose 23.5% between early September and 19 November on the Silicon Data index ([FourWeekMBA, Nov 2026](https://fourweekmba.com/ai-b200-rents-rose-23-5-since-august-as-gpu-credit-tightened/), [search summary]). Chips whose rents are rising are not chips nobody wants.

**The equity book has made money.** Cumulative unrealised gains on private holdings are $9.9bn against $2.2bn of losses and impairments, and public holdings added $14.6bn of net unrealised gains in nine months [filing] ([supplemental exhibit, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/supplementalfinancialinfor.htm)). Owning shares in a customer is not the same as lending it money to buy your product.

**Kress's caution is the proof that this is not Lucent.** Lucent's response to customers losing market access was to lend more. Nvidia's interim CEO has said in public that commitments "could be reduced or eliminated" if projects stall and that Nvidia will stay "disciplined and selective" (section 8). A vendor that walks away from weak credits is doing the opposite of what destroyed the telecom vendors. The mid-2026 SemiAnalysis view was that the backstop is a positive-sum device, turning neocloud credit into investment-grade-priced debt while giving Nvidia a share of the upside; coverage headlined it "Not Bearish on Nvidia" [estimate] ([Odaily](https://www.odaily.news/en/post/5211768), date not confirmed, pre-exit, [search summary]).

**The sell-side has not changed its view of demand.** After the report, Bank of America, UBS, Goldman Sachs, Wells Fargo and TD Cowen kept Buy-equivalent ratings; most cut price targets; Bernstein stayed at Market-Perform [estimate] ([CNBC, 18 Nov 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/18/analysts-react-to-nvidias-first-earnings-without-ceo-jensen-huang.html), [search summary]). Brad Gerstner and Gavin Baker, both shareholders, are reported to have urged the board to "avoid a rush to replace Jensen" [on record via secondary; originating outlet not identified] ([Yahoo Finance, Nov 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/nvidia-financing-role-neoclouds-faces-181433179.html), [search summary]).

Two honest limits on this case. First, the research found no broker note that sizes the guarantees against free cash flow or works through the Lucent numbers; the quantified defence rests on Nvidia's filings and on Wood. Second, one first-round note recorded Lynx Equity Strategies as putting the odds of an unwinding policy change at "next to nil", and a second-round search summary says Lynx "expects Kress to unwind Nvidia's 'circular' financing". Neither source could be opened, so Lynx's view is unresolved and should not be cited either way. A figure attributed to Moody's of Nvidia cash exceeding $240bn is excluded from this report because it does not fit the filings (cash and marketable debt securities were $53.5bn at 25 October).

The bull case is strongest on a narrow question, whether Nvidia can afford what it has promised. The answer on the filings is yes. It is weaker on the question bears ask, which is what happens to demand when the promises are rationed.

## 6. The bear case: the marginal buyer was created by Nvidia's credit, and the credit is being rationed

This section argues the case as its best defenders would. Serious bears do not claim the guarantees will bankrupt Nvidia. They claim that a meaningful slice of demand existed only because Nvidia's name made it financeable, and that the events of November 2026 are that support being withdrawn.

**The guarantor was the product.** Marc Rowan, whose firm Apollo is one of the six alliance partners and a lender in this market, said on CNBC on 17 November: "If that banking function, that guarantor function, gets taken away, access to capital for a large number of neoclouds is going to disappear" [on record; wording as carried by CNBC, video not checked] ([CNBC, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/17/apollo-ceo-marc-rowan-on-ai-debt.html), [search summary]). An investor quoted by Fortune after Huang's exit put it as: "If the capital providers now say that Nvidia guarantee is not worth what it was worth a week ago, that puts those neocloud businesses at risk" ([Fortune, 12 Nov 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/11/12/nvidia-neoclouds-jensen-huang-resignation/), pre-Q3, [search summary]). SemiAnalysis's post-exit piece argues that neoclouds raised money because Huang personally backed them and warns of a possible "large contraction" [opinion; piece not opened] ([SemiAnalysis, "Nvidia and the World Without Jensen", about 13 Nov 2026](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/nvidia-and-the-world-without-jensen), pre-Q3, [search summary]).

**The guarantee disappears exactly when a lender needs it.** Kress's own description is that the guarantee "doesn't exist anymore" if a project is delayed, and the Q3 exhibit says "certain commitments could be reduced or eliminated" if contingencies are not resolved in time ([TechCrunch, 18 Nov 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/18/nvidia-clarifies-when-it-will-and-wont-keep-financing-neoclouds/), [search summary]; [CFO commentary, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27cfocommentary.htm), [filing]). A construction lender is being asked to take the risk that the project runs late, and lateness is the event that voids the support. IFR's headline was that Nvidia "could walk away from guarantees, in blow to neoclouds" ([IFR, Nov 2026](https://www.ifre.com/bonds/2515910/nvidia-warns-it-could-walk-away-from-guarantees-in-blow-to-neoclouds), [search summary]). Morgan Stanley's credit analyst said the report "left the ecosystem support trajectory opaque" ([CNBC, 18 Nov 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/18/nvidia-neocloud-financing.html), [search summary]).

**Credit has already tightened.** Reported loan-to-value on GPU loans has fallen from as high as 80% to 50-60% with mid-to-high-teens rates (single source; section 1). CoreWeave, the strongest neocloud, said on 10 November "we will not use the unsecured debt market to fund our business at current yields"; its net interest expense of $846m was about four times its GAAP operating income of $207m ([Motley Fool transcript, 10 Nov 2026](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/11/10/coreweave-crwv-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/), pre-exit, [search summary]). CoreWeave and Nebius shares were each down more than 50% over three months ([CNBC, 17 Nov 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/17/apollo-ceo-marc-rowan-on-ai-debt.html), [search summary]). The $500bn platform that was supposed to replace Nvidia's balance sheet has funded nothing (section 1).

**The financing that is not in any tally: receivables.** Accounts receivable were **$81.0bn at 25 October 2026, against $38.5bn in January**, and days sales outstanding rose from 45 in Q1 to 60 in Q2 to 64 in Q3; the increase absorbed $42.6bn of operating cash flow in nine months; Nvidia cites "extended payment terms on large, multi-quarter agreements with certain investment-grade customers" [filing] ([CFO commentary](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27cfocommentary.htm) and [supplemental exhibit](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/supplementalfinancialinfor.htm), 17 Nov 2026). Letting a customer pay later is lending, and it sits on the balance sheet outside every guarantee count. The "53 days" in Nvidia's 2025 rebuttal is no longer the number.

**The commitments run both ways, and the bigger number faces suppliers.** Nvidia's own supply and capacity purchase commitments rose from $119bn (April) to $279bn (July) to **$390bn (October)** [filing] (same CFO commentary; earlier quarters via [Carrying Value, Nov 2026](https://carryingvalue.substack.com/p/nvidias-quarter-a-first-read-while), [search summary]). Those are firm obligations to buy components whether or not customers take the finished product. If financed demand slips, Nvidia is long inventory commitments on one side and short contingent guarantees on the other. Inventory is already $39.7bn, up from $21.4bn in January.

**Circularity at the top, not only the bottom.** Nvidia holds $30bn of OpenAI equity, guarantees up to $105bn of OpenAI's rent on a campus that exclusively hosts Nvidia compute, and has pledged about $10bn to Anthropic's IPO placement (sections 1 and 2). The indemnity that protects Nvidia on Ohio comes from OpenAI, which is the party whose insolvency triggers the guarantee; that weakness is this report's observation. Three direct customers were 14%, 11% and 11% of Q3 revenue and two were 18% and 17% of receivables [filing] (CFO commentary).

**Cash is going out faster than it comes in.** In nine months Nvidia spent $58.1bn buying equity securities and $73.5bn on buybacks and dividends, $131.6bn against $113.7bn of free cash flow (arithmetic on the Q3 exhibits). Cash and marketable debt securities are $53.5bn, less than half the maximum guarantee exposure. The bull replies that nothing is payable before fiscal 2029; the bear replies that the event that triggers the guarantee, an OpenAI default, is the same event that would collapse Nvidia's cash flow.

**Hidden price cuts.** Ben Thompson argued in August 2026 that residual-value support is functionally a price cut that never appears in the income statement [opinion; not opened] ([Stratechery, "Nvidia's Risky Business", about Aug 2026](https://stratechery.com/2026/nvidias-risky-business/), pre-exit, [search summary]). On this view, reported gross margin overstates the true economics of the marginal sale.

**Official-sector unease.** The IMF's Kristalina Georgieva on 16 November put AI "circular financing" at just over $1 trillion, about two-thirds debt-funded [on record via secondary] ([Invezz, 16 Nov 2026](https://invezz.com/nz/news/2026/11/16/ai-circular-financing-crosses-dollar1-trillion-imf-chief-flags-global-economy-risks/), pre-Q3, [search summary]). The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee flagged "circular arrangements" and limited transparency in AI financing in late September ([ANI, 30 Sep 2026](https://aninews.in/news/business/bank-of-england-says-ai-investment-boom-raises-financial-risks-debt-financing-could-reach-usd-41-trillion-by-203020260930181650/), pre-exit, [search summary]). Neither document was opened and neither is confirmed to name Nvidia.

Three items bears cite that this research could not stand behind. Ed Zitron's claim that "at least 200 billion dollars" of Nvidia chips sit in warehouses has no visible source and nothing in the filings corroborates it; the filings report only Nvidia's own inventory [unverified] ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/ai-critic-claims-200-billion-141420303.html), date not confirmed, [search summary]). Bank of America is reported to have counted about $230bn of guarantees in August 2026, against $108.5bn in Nvidia's filing for the same period; the basis is unknown and the two are unreconciled [unverified] ([Luminix](https://www.useluminix.com/reports/industry-analysis/ai-circular-deals), [search summary]). Michael Burry's positions are reported inconsistently: one account has him holding February 2027 $140 Nvidia puts, another says he disclosed no direct Nvidia position and holds puts on a semiconductor ETF and the Nasdaq-100 ([Benzinga, Nov 2026](https://www.benzinga.com/markets/tech/26/11/63184853/michael-burry-says-nvidia-lost-its-enforcer-don-has-fallen); [24/7 Wall St, 12 Nov 2026](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/11/12/michael-burry-says-nvidias-biggest-problem-isnt-jensen-huang/), [search summary]). His arguments (GPUs last two to three years, not five to six; the build-out is financed off balance sheet) are his claims, not established facts.

### Lucent and Nortel: what actually happened

In the late 1990s telecom equipment makers lent money to, or guaranteed the debts of, new carriers so that those carriers could buy equipment. Lucent's 10-K for the year to 30 September 2000 shows commitments to extend credit of about **$6.7bn, of which about $1.3bn was drawn, plus about $1.4bn of guarantees of customer debt** [filing] ([Lucent 10-K405, FY2000](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001006240/000095012300011855/y43690e10-k405.txt)). The two sum to $8.1bn, which is very likely the origin of the figure commentators quote, though no source states that derivation. When the high-yield market shut in 2000-2001 the carriers lost their other lenders, drew on the vendor, and failed. By 30 September 2001 Lucent had $3.0bn drawn and had reserved $2.1bn against it; provisions for bad debts and customer financings were $2,249m in fiscal 2001 and $1,253m in fiscal 2002; Winstar filed for Chapter 11 in April 2001 [filing] ([Lucent 10-K FY2002, Exhibit 13, 12 Dec 2002](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1006240/000095011702003045/ex13.htm)).

The financing losses were not what broke the companies. The revenue was. **Lucent's revenue fell from $28.9bn (fiscal 2000) to $12.3bn (fiscal 2002), down 57%; Nortel's fell from $27.9bn (2000) to $10.6bn (2002), down 62%** [filing; percentages are this report's arithmetic] (same Lucent exhibit; [Nortel 10-K FY2002, 10 Mar 2003](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/72911/000090956703000343/t09107e10vk.htm)). Lucent lost $14.2bn from continuing operations in fiscal 2001. Separately, the SEC charged Lucent and ten individuals in May 2004 with improperly recognising about $1.148bn of revenue in fiscal 2000; Lucent settled without admitting or denying and paid $25m [filing] ([SEC press release 2004-67, 17 May 2004](https://www.sec.gov/news/press/2004-67.htm)).

| | Lucent, Sep 2000 | Nvidia, Oct 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Customer support outstanding | $8.1bn ($6.7bn credit commitments + $1.4bn guarantees) | $150bn ($118bn guarantees + $32bn neocloud agreements) |
| As % of revenue | 24% of $33.6bn as originally reported; 28% of $28.9bn as restated | 32% of annualised Q3 revenue ($463bn) |
| Form | Loans and loan commitments, drawn in cash | Contingent guarantees and capacity commitments; no cash paid |
| Earliest cash exposure | Immediate | Fiscal 2029 for the Ohio guarantee |
| Largest customers | Leveraged new carriers | Hyperscalers (56% of data center revenue) |
| Vendor's free cash flow | Not comparable | $43.8bn in the latest quarter |
| Response when customers lost market access | Lent more | Says commitments may be reduced |

Sources: Lucent filings above; Lucent's $33.6bn originally reported revenue via [Tunguz, Oct 2025](https://tomtunguz.com/nvidia_nortel_vendor_financing_comparison/) [estimate; not reconciled to the filing]; Nvidia figures from the [Q3 FY27 CFO commentary](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27cfocommentary.htm). Ratios are this report's arithmetic. Other analysts get different ratios because they count different things: Tunguz put Nvidia's direct investments at about 67% of trailing revenue against Lucent's 24%; Navnoor Bawa's commitments-and-guarantees measure gives 22.8% against 24.7% ([Navnoor Bawa Research](https://www.navnoorbawaresearch.com/p/nvidias-credit-book-outgrew-its-cash), date not confirmed, [search summary]).

**Where the analogy holds.** The incentive is the same: a supplier that funds or owns its customer has reason to keep shipping regardless of the customer's economics. The dependence is the same: the customers at the margin cannot buy without outside credit, and when that credit retreats the vendor must choose between extending more support and losing the sale. The opacity is similar: much of the support is off balance sheet and disclosed in aggregate. And the revenue lesson transfers directly, because it did not depend on the vendor's own losses: when the carriers lost access to capital, equipment revenue fell by more than half.

**Where it breaks.** Lucent's support was loans drawn in cash before default; Nvidia's is contingent, mostly starts in fiscal 2029, and has cost nothing so far. Lucent's biggest buyers were the weak credits; Nvidia's biggest are the strongest companies in the world. Lucent could not fund its book from cash flow; Nvidia can, several times over. Lucent's case included revenue manipulation, and nothing of that kind has been alleged or established against Nvidia. And Nvidia's management is pulling back where Lucent's pushed on.

One difference cuts against the bulls. Fibre and switches lasted decades; GPUs are depreciated over four to six years and financed on three-year amortising loans, so lenders have less time to be wrong ([AM Compute](https://www.amcompute.com/gpu-depreciation-schedules), date not confirmed, [search summary]).

The fair reading is that the analogy fails as a prediction about Nvidia's solvency and holds as a description of how revenue behaves when a vendor's weakest customers lose financing. How much of Nvidia's revenue those customers represent is the subject of section 2.

### Step 2: what does a delay tie up?

The simplest model: capital waiting to earn = annual capex × months of delay ÷ 12. All figures below are this report's arithmetic.

| 2027 capex base | 1 month | 2 months | 3 months | 6 months |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $920bn | $77bn | $153bn | $230bn | $460bn |
| $1.1tn | $92bn | $183bn | $275bn | $550bn |
| $1.32tn | $110bn | $220bn | $330bn | $660bn |
| $1.6tn | $133bn | $267bn | $400bn | $800bn |

A cross-check from actual spending: the largest builders spent about $232bn on property and equipment in their latest reported quarters (Amazon $77.3bn, Alphabet $51.4bn, Microsoft $39.4bn cash, Meta $34.9bn, Oracle $28.5bn), and about $259bn with CoreWeave and SpaceX, which is roughly $86bn a month already, before any 2027 growth [filing; sums are arithmetic] ([Amazon 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001018724/000101872426000040/amzn-20260930.htm); [Alphabet 8-K exhibit, 28 Oct 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001652044/000165204426000080/googexhibit991q32026.htm); [Microsoft 8-K exhibit, 28 Oct 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/789019/000119312526436122/msft-ex99_1.htm); [Oracle 10-Q, 11 Sep 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526389274/orcl-20260831.htm); [SpaceX 8-K exhibit, 20 Oct 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1181412/000162828026066482/spaceexplorationtechnologi.htm), all pre-exit).

So on any current estimate, two to three months of delay across the whole base is $150bn to $400bn. **"Hundreds of billions" is arithmetically plausible.** It rests on two assumptions that nobody has measured.

The first is that the delay applies to the whole spending base. Only part of capex is paid before a site is energised: chips, long-lead electrical equipment, deposits. No source splits 2027 capex into chips, other equipment and buildings, or says how much is paid before revenue starts. On chips alone the number is smaller: Nvidia's data center revenue is about $36bn a month ($107.6bn ÷ 3), so a three-month slip ties up about $108bn and reaches "hundreds of billions" only beyond a quarter, or once other chip vendors and memory are added (arithmetic).

The second is that the delay is new, on top of today's lag. Some lag already exists and is already financed. The question is whether it is lengthening. The evidence that it is, is circumstantial but broad. Microsoft's capital spending came in about $5bn below its own outlook of more than $50bn for the September quarter because leased capacity was not ready, and it expects spending to accelerate in early 2027 partly because of "delayed deliveries" [on record via transcript not opened] ([Motley Fool transcript, 29 Oct 2026](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/29/microsoft-msft-q1-2027-earnings-call-transcript/), pre-exit, [search summary]); that is about 10% of one quarter slipping by at least a quarter. Goldman Sachs estimated in May 2026 that only 50-60% of planned capacity would come online on schedule over two years ([Wikipedia, "AI build-out financing"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_build-out_financing), retrieved 22 Nov 2026, tertiary). Nebius, IREN and Nscale have all disclosed slipped sites (section 3). Against that, CoreWeave's third-quarter capex overshot guidance because deliveries arrived sooner than planned. No one has published a measured industry figure in months from paying for a chip to earning revenue on it.

Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta together generated about $105bn of trailing free cash flow; Amazon and Oracle were negative by about $71bn. The five largest builders therefore have roughly $34bn of aggregate free cash flow against a potential $230-400bn timing requirement (arithmetic). A delay of that size cannot be met from cash flow. It would be met from cash balances, about $560bn across the five (of which $75bn at Alphabet is restricted SpaceX stock), and from new debt. A strategist's projection dated 20 November has capital budgets at 116% of operating cash flow this year and 126% next, before any delay [estimate] ([SanCap, 20 Nov 2026](https://portfolio-strategy.apsec.com/2026/11/20/out-of-consensus-views-on-markets-for-2027/), [search summary]).

The labs sit apart. Anthropic's final prospectus shows commitments of "approximately $900.9 billion, substantially all of which is related to compute and infrastructure" [filing] ([Anthropic 424B4, 19 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026074944/anthropicpbc-424b4.htm)), but it has just raised about $107bn and has contractual rights to terminate where suppliers miss delivery dates. OpenAI cut a funding round from $30bn or more to about $20bn, with a first close of about $11bn at a $1.25tn valuation [anonymous] ([Yahoo Finance/Reuters, 6 Nov 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/openai-closes-11-billion-funding-213757127.html), pre-exit, [search summary]); no report of late payment or renegotiated contracts was found. Its pressure shows up at its counterparties. The neoclouds cannot fund a delay at all (section 3).

### Verdict

**Is "hundreds of billions" plausible?** Yes as an order of magnitude, conditional on the delay being broad and incremental. A three-month slip on $0.9-1.6tn of 2027 capex is $230-400bn of capital waiting to earn. It is his own unsourced estimate, and the two conditions are unproven.

**Is "working capital" the right term?** Loosely. Chips and buildings awaiting power are capital assets under construction, not inventory or receivables. The pieces that are working capital in the strict sense are visible elsewhere in the chain: Nvidia's receivables stretching from 45 to 64 days (about $24bn of extra credit extended in two quarters, on this research's arithmetic), Amazon's supplier payment terms of "up to one year", Oracle's $11.4bn of customer prepayments in one quarter, SpaceX's rent in advance. The gap is being passed around the chain according to bargaining power.

**Is "shortage" the right word?** Only for part of the market. Three different things are being called a shortage: a timing gap (cash out before cash in), which is what his sentence describes; a cost-of-capital problem; and rationing, where a borrower cannot raise at any sensible price. In the first three weeks of November, during a sell-off, AI-related issuers raised more than $200bn: Microsoft $25bn, Meta $15bn plus €9bn plus equity, Oracle $10bn of notes and $10bn of shares, SpaceX about $44bn, Anthropic about $107bn, OpenAI $11-15bn (sum is arithmetic on the sources above and in sections 4 and 12). That is not a shortage for large issuers. It is a price gradient, from 4.85% for Microsoft through 7.0-8.65% for Oracle and about 8.3% for SpaceX's leases to roughly 15% for CoreWeave's unsecured debt, with the window shut for the weakest. And the economic cost of a short delay is an order of magnitude smaller than the cash requirement: $300bn idle for a quarter at 8% costs about $6bn of interest (arithmetic).

Named views bracket this. Apollo's chief economist Torsten Slok said "at the moment we have a shortage of capital"; Bank of America's analysts said capital "remains ample for investment grade firms and projects backstopped by suppliers" (both via [Fortune, 2 Nov 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/11/02/ai-boom-supply-crunch-capital-debt-bonds-capex-spending/), pre-exit, [search summary]). The second half of the Bank of America sentence is the link to this report: "backstopped by suppliers" is what is now in question.

## 8. Kress's stance: tighter words, an unchanged book, and a quarter that closed before she took over

### What she has said in public

Colette Kress has been CFO since 2013 and became interim CEO when the board accepted Huang's resignation on 11 November 2026, announced the next day [filing] ([Nvidia 8-K, filed 12 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000082/nvda-20261111.htm)). ## 9. The SEC history: one quarter borrowed from a supplier in 2000, a non-fraud order against the company, a fraud charge against its CFO

Nvidia has been the subject of three SEC accounting or disclosure matters in about 25 years. Two ended in settled orders against the company, one ended with no action. The precise legal characterisation matters, because headlines now pair the current probe with this history and get it wrong in both directions. This section draws on the SEC's own documents; the complaint against the former CFO and the 2022 order were read as raw text, and the 2003 company order was read through a tool that returns excerpts, so quotations from that order should be checked against the live page before being reproduced.

### What happened in 2000

In early 2000 Nvidia's internal forecasts showed it would miss consensus expectations for the quarter ending 30 April 2000, the first quarter of its fiscal 2001. The SEC's complaint says "senior management immediately focused the Company on reducing costs for the quarter in order to bolster gross margins and net income", and that the CFO, Christine B. Hoberg, "directed that the amount of cost savings from Nvidia's supplier needed to be approximately $3 million" [filing] ([SEC complaint, *SEC v. Hoberg*, N.D. Cal., 11 Sep 2003](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf)).

The supplier, one of the companies that manufactured and packaged Nvidia's chips and never named in either SEC document, proposed on 30 March 2000 what it called a "cost transfer": it would credit Nvidia about $3m in the current quarter, and Nvidia would "re-pay" it in August 2000 through higher prices. Hoberg's emailed reply is the centre of the case: "can not sign this or have this in print. Will wipe out the credit in Q1. [N]eed to arrange this separately and trust us to abide by it." The deal was then split into two letters that did not refer to each other. On 3 April Nvidia agreed to pay higher prices in August totalling about $3.3m (the company order gives $3,299,000); on 4 April the supplier granted about $3.3m of credits for the April quarter. Nvidia booked the credit and not the obligation. When the auditors reviewed the quarter in May 2000, they were shown the 4 April letter and not the 3 April one (same complaint).

Under GAAP the promise to repay was a liability that offset the credit. Leaving it out overstated the quarter's gross profit by 6.4%, net income by 15.3% and diluted earnings per share by 14.8%, and let the reported results beat consensus on gross margin and earnings [filing] ([SEC order, Release 34-48480, 11 Sep 2003](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-48480.htm)). Reported revenue for that quarter was $148.5m and net income $18.3m ([Nvidia 10-Q, 14 Jun 2000](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000101287000003358/0001012870-00-003358-0001.txt)). In economic terms the transaction was a one-quarter loan of margin from a supplier. A footnote to the SEC order records a twist: the audit committee found that the higher August prices "were not, in fact, paid", so the supplier was never repaid, which does not cure the accounting for the April quarter.

### The inquiry, the restatement and its size

SEC staff told Nvidia in January 2002 that it had concerns about certain accounting matters. Nvidia announced the inquiry and an internal review on 14 February 2002. The audit committee, assisted by Cooley Godward and forensic accountants from KPMG (which was also Nvidia's auditor), finished on 29 April 2002. That day Nvidia announced it would restate, and announced separately that Hoberg was taking a leave of absence; she resigned on 31 July 2002 [filing] ([Nvidia press release, 29 Apr 2002](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000095016802001116/dex991.htm); [Nvidia 10-K for fiscal 2002, 14 May 2002](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000101287002002262/d10k.htm)).

On the periods restated, the two primary sources word it differently and both are given here. Nvidia: "fiscal 2000, 2001 and the first three quarters of fiscal 2002", which matches the reader's lead. The SEC order: "the last three quarters of fiscal year 2000, fiscal year 2001, and fiscal year 2002". The restated figures were filed in the fiscal 2002 10-K on 14 May 2002; no amended 10-K or 10-Q for the restated periods appears on EDGAR.

The restatement was small and mostly about timing between periods: manufacturing costs, inventory write-downs, accruals and deferred revenue. Annual revenue for fiscal 2000 and 2001 did not change. Net income for fiscal 2000 rose from $38.1m to $41.0m; fiscal 2001 fell from $99.9m to $98.5m; fiscal 2002 moved from $177.1m to $176.9m. The SEC's own footnote: "the Company's total net income for the entire period increased by approximately $1.3 million." (A preliminary press-release version showed a $3.7m cut to fiscal 2001; the audited figure is $1.5m.) The harm the SEC identified was to one quarter's reported margin and earnings relative to expectations.

**Is "fraud" the right word for the company's settlement? No.** The company order contains no antifraud charge and no penalty. Nvidia's own description is an order on "non-fraud financial reporting, books and records, and internal control provisions". Saying "Nvidia settled SEC fraud charges in 2003" or "Nvidia was fined" is wrong. It is equally wrong to call it a technical bookkeeping matter: the same order says "Nvidia engaged in accounting misconduct intended to boost the Company's financial results" and that the 10-Q and press release were "materially false and misleading". The accurate one-line version is that the company reached a non-fraud settlement over conduct the SEC called fraud when it charged the individual responsible. And for Hoberg, "fraud" is what the SEC alleged; she settled without admitting or denying, so she was not found to have committed fraud after a trial. No one else was charged. The complaint refers to "other senior executives" who ensured the cost-savings request matched the forecast shortfall, and names none. About 13 securities class actions and three derivative suits were all dismissed during 2003 with no settlement payment disclosed.

### The 2022 crypto settlement

On 6 May 2022 the SEC issued a settled order finding that Nvidia's 10-Qs for the second and third quarters of fiscal 2018 failed to disclose that cryptocurrency mining was "a significant factor in the year-over-year growth in revenue" from gaming GPUs, when analysts "routinely asked senior management" about exactly that. The violations were of Securities Act Sections 17(a)(2) and (3), Exchange Act Section 13(a) and related rules, and the disclosure-controls rule 13a-15(a). Nvidia paid **$5.5m** without admitting or denying [filing] ([SEC order 33-11060, 6 May 2022](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2022/33-11060.pdf)). The reader's lead is confirmed.

Three features matter. The numbers were right; the failure was in the narrative explanation of what drove them, so there was no restatement. No individual was charged. And the order itself says the Section 17(a)(2) and (3) violations do "not require scienter and may rest on a finding of negligence", so "fraud" is again imprecise; the SEC's headline was "Inadequate Disclosures". The related shareholder class action is still alive: a class was certified on 25 March 2026 and no settlement had been disclosed as of the August 2026 10-Q ([Nvidia 10-Q, 26 Aug 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000075/nvda-20260726.htm)). The Q3 exhibits show $107m of "legal settlement costs" with no statement of which matter; this report does not attribute it.

There was a third episode. In 2006 Nvidia restated for stock-option measurement dates ($127.4m of non-cash charges net of tax); the SEC opened an inquiry in August 2006 and closed it on 26 October 2007 with "no enforcement action" recommended ([Nvidia 10-K for fiscal 2008, 21 Mar 2008](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581008000011/fy2008annualreportonform10k.htm)).

### Does the history bear on today?

Partly. Four things carry over, and four do not.

What carries over. First, the charging pattern: both times the SEC charged the company under provisions that require no intent and reserved fraud for an individual. Second, Nvidia is now a repeat respondent on periodic-reporting provisions. The 2022 order directs it to cease and desist from future violations of Section 13(a) and the disclosure-controls rule, and the no-penalty outcome of 2003 rested expressly on prompt remediation and cooperation, a leniency that is harder to earn a third time. Third, continuity of people on the 2022 matter: the same CEO (Huang), the same CFO (Kress, since 2013) and the same auditor (PwC, since 2004), though the order named no individual. Fourth, and most relevant, the substance of 2022: a failure to explain the quality and sustainability of a demand driver that investors were asking about. If today's questions concern how much AI revenue is supported by Nvidia's own financing, guarantees, investments or extended payment terms, the "known trends" disclosure theory the SEC used in 2022 is the closest precedent. That parallel is this report's analysis; no source asserts it.

What is only colour. The 2000 conduct is 26 years old, involved about $3.3m in one quarter, a different CFO, a different auditor (KPMG) and a company with under $1bn of revenue before Sarbanes-Oxley; the restatement raised cumulative income. Neither order found intent by the company and neither named Huang or Kress. The $5.5m penalty conveys precedent, not magnitude. And the 2006-07 episode shows that an SEC inquiry at this company, alongside a nine-figure restatement, has ended with no action.

The fair sentence is this: Nvidia has twice settled SEC disclosure or accounting cases without admitting wrongdoing, in neither case on intent-based fraud charges against the company; the more recent concerned inadequate disclosure of what was driving revenue growth, under the same CFO and auditor as today. What is on the record this month (an immediate CEO resignation, a board committee investigation and a withheld 10-Q) says far more about the present situation than the history does. Whether the Reuters article of 20 November itself recounts this history could not be confirmed.

### Every earlier instance of a report filed after the release

The table gives a different picture from the one in the press. **In 22 of the 42 completed periods since fiscal 2017 the periodic report was filed after the earnings release.** They fall into three groups.

*Before August 2018, always.* For the nine periods from Q1 FY2017 to Q1 FY2019 the 10-Q came 12 to 14 days after earnings every time and the 10-K 20 days after. That was simply Nvidia's routine. Same-day filing began with Q2 FY2019 on 16 August 2018. Further back, from fiscal 2004 to 2016, 10-Qs came 8 to 26 days after earnings. The lead's premise that Nvidia "normally" files with results is true only of recent years.

*Since August 2018, thirteen times: eight 10-Qs and five 10-Ks.* No Nvidia filing states a reason for any of them. The context below is coincidence in time, not stated cause.

A two-day lag was Nvidia's norm from August 2021 to August 2023. The largest 10-Q gap since same-day filing began is seven days (Q2 FY2023 and Q1 FY2025). Major events that did *not* slow the report include the crypto-inventory quarter of November 2018, the Mellanox closing, the Arm agreement and the October 2023 export rules.

*The streak that just ended.* The nine periodic reports before this one, from August 2024 to August 2026, were all filed the same day as earnings, within about 45 minutes of the 8-K. That is the practice Nvidia broke.

So what is unusual today is not the gap, which stood at five days on 22 November. It is that Nvidia announced the gap in advance, said "weeks", and furnished a substitute exhibit. If the 10-Q is filed on the deadline the gap will be 17 days, the longest since December 2010 but shorter than gaps that were once routine. The August 2022 episode shows Nvidia has held a 10-Q for a week to add disclosure about a late development, with no change to reported numbers. That is the closest in-house analogue to the explanation attributed to UBS.

### NT 10-Q and NT 10-K filings

A Form 12b-25, known as an "NT" filing, is the notice a company must file when it will miss a periodic-report deadline. Nvidia has filed exactly two in 28 years on EDGAR, out of 2,494 filings, and none since 2006 [filing] (EDGAR feed).

The first, an NT 10-K on 29 April 2002, gave as its reason that Nvidia "is in the process of restating previously reported financial results"; the 10-K followed 15 days later, inside the extension ([Form 12b-25, 29 Apr 2002](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000095016802001121/dnt10k.htm)). That is the episode in section 9. The second, an NT 10-Q on 11 September 2006, arose from the audit committee's review of stock-option dating; the 10-Q arrived 82 days late, on 29 November 2006, with a restatement of $199.6m pre-tax, after a Nasdaq deficiency letter ([Form 12b-25, 11 Sep 2006](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000119312506188313/dnt10q.htm); [Nvidia 10-Q, 29 Nov 2006](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000119312506243229/d10q.htm)). Both of Nvidia's genuinely late filings therefore shared today's ingredients, a board-level review and regulator interest, and both ended in restatements. They differ from today in one decisive respect: in both, the company said the accounting itself was under review. Nothing Nvidia has filed says that now.

### How other large companies behaved

Two kinds of precedent exist and they should not be mixed. Dates come from each company's EDGAR feed [filing]; all predate November 2026.

**On time, but later than usual.** This is Nvidia's current category, and the clean comparisons are few.

| Company | Normal gap | The episode | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mattel, Q3 2019 | 0-1 day | After a whistleblower letter and an audit committee investigation, [earnings on 29 Oct 2019](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/63276/000119312519277846/d788142d8k.htm) came with a non-reliance notice and a CFO departure; the [10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/63276/000162828019013987/mat9301910-q.htm) followed 14 days later, on the last timely day | Restated two quarters of 2017 ($109m of tax expense in the wrong quarter); material weaknesses; investigation "did not find that management engaged in fraud" |
| Macy's, Q3 2024 | 3-14 days | Delayed the earnings release itself; [preliminary sales on 25 Nov 2024](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/794367/000079436724000199/n-20241125.htm), full results 11 Dec, [10-Q on 12 Dec](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/794367/000162828024051058/m-20241102.htm), exactly day 40 | One employee hid about $151m of delivery expenses; immaterial revision, not a restatement; material weakness |
| Nvidia, Q2 FY2023 | 0-2 days | 10-Q seven days after earnings, 73 seconds after an export-control 8-K | Added disclosure only; numbers unchanged |
| Under Armour (2019), Nikola (2020-21), Boeing (2019-20), GE (2017-18) | Varies | External investigations were public | Filing timing did not change at all |

**Late, with an NT filing, during an investigation or review.** In a hand-picked sample of 14 such episodes, at least 11 ended with restated or corrected prior-period figures. That is not a base rate; the list was built from well-known problem cases. Delays ranged from days to more than a year: [Dell](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/826083/000095013406017769/d39660ntnt10vq.htm) 412 days (2006-07, SEC and audit committee investigations, restated four years); Marvell 315 days (2015-16, auditor resigned); Super Micro 180 days (2024-25, auditor resigned, no restatement); Apple 141 days (2006, option dating, restated); Kraft Heinz 100 days (2019, restated about $181m); ADM within its extensions twice (2024, segment data restated).

Two lessons carry over. First, early reassurance had little predictive value. Kraft Heinz's notice said it did "not expect matters subject to the investigation to be material", and ten weeks later it restated ([Kraft Heinz NT 10-K, 28 Feb 2019](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1637459/000163745919000019/nt10-k.htm); [8-K, 6 May 2019](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1637459/000163745919000033/may2019form8-k.htm)). Super Micro's said it did not anticipate "any material changes to its results", and that proved right ([Super Micro NT 10-K, 30 Aug 2024](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1375365/000137536524000031/smci-form12bx25nt10xkx2024.htm); [10-K, 25 Feb 2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1375365/000137536525000004/smci-20240630.htm)). Kraft Heinz was a same-day or next-day filer before its episode, so its first visible sign was the same as Nvidia's: earnings without the report. Second, the split in the first table is informative. Where the investigation was internal and about the books (Mattel, Macy's), a normally fast filer slowed down, used the full 40 days, and filed with corrections and a material weakness. Where the investigation was external or unrelated to the books, timing did not change. Nvidia's deliberate, pre-announced slowdown resembles the first group in form. But Mattel and Macy's each said at the outset that accounting was the issue, and Nvidia has not.

One tell follows from this: whether the 10-Q lands well before 4 December, as in August 2022, or on 4 December itself, as with Mattel and Macy's.

### The deadline, and what happens if Nvidia misses it

Nvidia is a large accelerated filer, so its 10-Q is due "40 days after the end of the fiscal quarter" ([SEC Form 10-Q general instructions](https://www.sec.gov/files/form10-q.pdf)). Forty days after 25 October 2026 is **Friday 4 December 2026**, with no weekend or holiday adjustment. The lead's date is correct.

If Nvidia misses it, the sequence is as follows; the dates are this report's arithmetic on SEC staff guidance ([Exchange Act Rules C&DI, Q135](https://www.sec.gov/rules-regulations/staff-guidance/compliance-disclosure-interpretations/exchange-act-rules)).

| Step | Date | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| File Form 12b-25 (NT 10-Q) | By Monday 7 Dec 2026 | Must "state… in reasonable detail why" the report is late, and say whether results will change significantly. This would be the first on-record explanation |
| Extended deadline | Wednesday 9 Dec 2026 | A 10-Q filed by then is treated as timely. "There are no additional extensions" |
| Beyond 9 Dec | | Loss of eligibility to use Form S-3, the short-form registration, for about twelve months. Nvidia sold $25bn of notes off an automatic shelf in June 2026 ([Nvidia 8-K, 18 Jun 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000119312526275783/d48176d8k.htm)) |
| | | A Nasdaq deficiency letter under Listing Rule 5250(c)(1): typically 60 days to submit a plan and up to 180 days to cure, with no immediate effect on trading. Nvidia received one in 2006 on the day it filed its NT |
| | | Insider sales under Rule 144 are restricted while the company is not current |
| | | Under the 2016 notes indenture, reports go to the trustee within 15 days of the SEC due date; a breach becomes an event of default only after notice from the trustee or 25% of holders and a further 90 days. On a literal reading that is at least three and a half months away, and it is not a legal opinion ([Base Indenture, 16 Sep 2016](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000119312516711700/d260668dex41.htm)) |

None of this applies yet. Nvidia had no commercial paper outstanding at 26 July 2026, so a short delay creates no obvious funding problem.

What the delay means cannot be read from the delay. The on-record facts fit a disclosure-drafting explanation: full GAAP financial statements were released (unaudited, as quarterly figures always are), and what is being held is narrative and certification. The precedents say that explanation is sometimes all there is (Nvidia in 2022) and sometimes the first sign of more (Kraft Heinz). Section 11 assigns rough odds.

#### The four changes that stand out

**One filing carries most of them.** The 10-Q filed on 19 November 2025 introduced the first guarantee, the first investment-commitment figure, the geographic re-basing that removed Singapore, the widened definition of direct customers, and the "regulatory and government inquiries and investigations" wording. It came about ten weeks after SEC disclosure staff closed their review of the FY2025 10-K, so none of these changes was prompted by any public comment letter.

**Singapore.** On the old basis Singapore was 18% of FY2025 revenue ($23.7bn) and 22% of the July 2025 quarter. Nvidia's footnotes said customers "use Singapore to centralize invoicing while our products are almost always shipped elsewhere", that shipments to Singapore were under 2% of FY2025 revenue, and that "over 99% of controlled Data Center compute revenue billed to Singapore was for orders from U.S.-based customers". The switch to customer headquarters was announced in a table footnote, with prior periods recast, on the stated ground that it "provides a better representation of the geographic profile of our revenue". The reader's lead on this is confirmed. Accounting rules permit either basis if disclosed.

The single most useful fact for anyone examining this is in the recast itself. Moving to headquarters reallocated the Singapore amount mostly to the United States, but it also **raised the China figure for FY2025 from $17.1bn to $25.0bn**, and for the April 2025 quarter from $5.5bn to $9.7bn (this research's comparison of the two bases; the differences sum to zero, so it is a pure reallocation). So part of what was billed to Singapore and "other" countries belonged to China-headquartered groups. That does not contradict the 99% statement, which covered only export-controlled data center chips in two quarters. The filings do not say what the uplift consists of. Two further footnotes, including Nvidia's estimate of how much Taiwan-billed revenue served US and European end customers, were removed in May 2026. A figure attributed to the Financial Times, that Nvidia's Singapore subsidiary booked about $104bn of revenue in 2025 and "roughly half" of sales, measures a legal entity's accounts and is not in conflict with the 18% to 22% billing-location figures. That figure surfaced only in a search summary whose source page could not be identified, so it is [unverified]; a related Fortune piece was seen as a headline only ([Fortune, 12 Nov 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/11/12/nvidia-changed-earnings-report-format-singapore-revenue-megaspeed-probe-colette-kress/), pre-Q3).

**Financing arrangements and payment terms.** The sequence of statements is: 25 February 2026, "We have not entered into any financing arrangements"; 20 May 2026, sentence unchanged and no mention of extended terms; 26 August 2026, "we have and may in the future provide longer payment terms ranging from 90 days up to one year". The filings do not say when the first such terms were granted. DSO was still 45 days at 26 April 2026, which suggests little effect before the July quarter. Had DSO stayed at 45 days, October receivables would have been about $58bn against the reported $81bn (arithmetic). The same August 10-Q that introduced the longer terms, the $36bn of capacity commitments and the $105bn guarantee states that there were "no material changes to our significant accounting policies".

**How the neocloud sales are accounted for.** Nvidia sells hardware to a neocloud and commits to buy back the capacity that hardware produces. It says it "will earn revenue on the upfront sale". In 2023 SEC staff asked a smaller version of this question, about cloud services bundled with hardware, and Nvidia replied that it would revise its policy note "if and when such multi-element arrangements are material" ([Nvidia response, 29 Jun 2023](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581023000160/filename1.htm)). The policy note has not been expanded to describe the neocloud arrangements. That is a disclosure observation, not a finding of error.

#### The comment letters

EDGAR holds 17 staff letters and responses since 2016, forming five exchanges: sanctions-country contacts (2017); crypto revenue in the segment note (2019), where staff closed the review three years before the Enforcement Division's 2022 order on the same subject; board risk oversight in the proxy (2022); MD&A quantification and cloud-services revenue recognition (2023); and the non-GAAP treatment of the $4.5bn H20 charge, licence revenue and the segment table ([SEC staff letter, 22 Jul 2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000000000025007684/filename1.pdf)). None asks about bill-and-hold, geography or Singapore, concentration, receivables, purchase commitments or investments. The last public letter is the completion letter of 10 September 2025. Letters are released only after a review closes, so an open review would not be visible; and disclosure reviews are a separate process from enforcement investigations.

One exchange sits awkwardly with a later filing. On 31 July 2025 Nvidia told staff, in defending the exclusion of the H20 charge, that "there is not a market outside of China for H20" ([Nvidia response](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581025000192/filename1.htm)). Four weeks later its 10-Q reported "approximately $650 million of H20 revenue from sales to an unrestricted customer outside of China, resulting in a $180 million release of previously reserved H20 inventory". The innocent reading is that one opportunistic sale is not a market, and staff closed the review two weeks after that 10-Q.

Why the first hypothesis ranks highest: it is the only one that all three news reports gesture towards, it matches the SEC's two previous cases against Nvidia in kind (disclosure and controls, not fabricated numbers), and it connects to the event that actually triggered the board investigation. Why it is not higher: both Reuters and the NYT say explicitly that they do not know whether geography is involved, and "accounting" in the Reuters headline is a broader word than "disclosure".

#### The innocent explanations, and how likely they are

**The inquiry is a routine response to a CEO's sudden exit amid press allegations, and ends with no charges.** When a founder-CEO leaves abruptly and the board cites lack of candor, SEC staff opening a file is close to automatic. The NYT's sources called it early-stage. This report's judgment is that **no enforcement action against the company on accounting grounds is the single most likely outcome, perhaps 50-60%**, with a negotiated non-fraud order on disclosure or controls the next most likely, and a restatement of reported results unlikely on present evidence. That judgment rests on the absence of any allegation about the numbers, on the historical charging pattern in section 9, and on base rates the research could not quantify.

**The 10-Q delay is routine caution.** Kress became interim CEO six days before the report and must certify the filing in two capacities. A board committee with outside counsel is mid-investigation. A state subpoena, congressional requests, a class action and the SEC reports all arrived within two weeks and all need describing in risk factors and legal proceedings. Nvidia held a 10-Q for a week in 2022 to add an export-control disclosure. The UBS paraphrase says the delay concerns risk-factor disclosure about the Huang investigation. On that evidence **a disclosure-drafting explanation, with no change to the numbers already published, is likely: perhaps two chances in three.** The residual splits between a delay that brings materially new disclosure (new contingencies, a controls qualification, more on guarantees) and, least likely, one that changes reported figures. The second cannot be excluded: Kraft Heinz looked like this at the same stage.

**The filing changes each have a mundane reason.** Geographic reporting by invoice address had become uninformative; commitments tables were redesigned because the commitments changed in nature; customer labels were dropped because the largest customers rotate. Each is plausible. The difficulty for Nvidia is cumulative: several changes that each reduced comparability arrived together in November 2025, just as the financing programme began.

What would discriminate between these readings is in the 10-Q itself: whether the litigation note names an SEC matter, a formal order or a subpoena; whether the disclosure-controls conclusion still says "effective"; whether the contingencies paragraph still says outcomes "will not have a material adverse effect"; how the $9.5bn guarantee line and the Ohio guarantees are classified and valued; whether the policy note finally describes the neocloud arrangements; and what the $107m of legal settlement costs was for.

## 12. Oracle: "fully funded" means a $20bn cap on market raises, with the rest resting on vendors, customers and landlords

Oracle matters to this story as the largest single intermediary between OpenAI and the chip supply chain. Its contract with OpenAI is reported at $300bn of computing power over five years [unverified; WSJ, 10 Sep 2025, via [Wikipedia, "OpenAI"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI)]; Oracle's own 10-K and 10-Q never name OpenAI. ### (a) What Oracle said at Investor Day about funding

Investor Day was on 28 October 2026 in Las Vegas, with co-CEOs Clay Magouyrk and Mike Sicilia and CFO Hilary Maxson presenting. The release furnished to the SEC that day contains the funding plan in one paragraph: "Oracle committed to limiting additional funding to be raised in the capital markets for its FY27-FY30 investment cycle to $20 billion, currently expected to be $10 billion in equity and $10 billion in gross debt. But the second candidate is the more useful way to think about the claim that followed: "fully funded" is true only if roughly $30bn or more of non-market funding arrives. One caveat: a slide or an answer in Q&A could have quantified a residual need, and those were not readable.

### (c) Who the vendors are, and whether Nvidia is one

Oracle has named no vendor, amount or structure. At Investor Day it reportedly said it did not think "that we said that we have specific financing with any specific vendor or in what magnitude" (transcript, [search summary]). In Oracle's audited and reviewed filings through 31 August 2026 the words "vendor financing" do not appear at all; the nearest items are small: "short-term financing related to capital expenditures" and $6.2bn of unpaid capital expenditures. The phrase first appears in the November offering document: "we expect to incur additional borrowings or other liabilities under vendor financing, leases and other financing arrangements" ([Oracle 424B2, 2 Nov 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526445846/d181336d424b2.htm)). Oracle's own leverage definition counts vendor financing as debt.

**Is Nvidia one of them? Probably, and unconfirmed.** Nvidia is Oracle's main chip supplier. Nvidia's Q3 exhibits describe the two mechanisms a chip vendor would use: "hardware financing arrangements" within a new $9.5bn guarantee line, and "extended payment terms on large, multi-quarter agreements with certain investment-grade customers". Oracle is investment grade and is taking large multi-quarter Rubin deliveries, so it fits the description. Neither disclosure names Oracle, and no report names the counterparties. Commentators name Nvidia only as an example. Nvidia is also a named Oracle customer ([Oracle term sheet, 1 Feb 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526032650/d36096dfwp.htm)), so the relationship runs both ways.

"Further private discussions with vendors" concedes that talks continue. They could cover longer deferrals, vendor-arranged leasing of chips, vendor guarantees of landlord debt, or a vendor taking over leases. Those are possibilities consistent with the wording, not reported facts.

**Does Kress's tighter stance affect Oracle?** Indirectly, and conditionally. As summarised from the 17 November call, Nvidia will be selective and "investment-grade customers and prepayments get preference" ([SiliconANGLE, 19 Nov 2026](https://siliconangle.com/2026/11/19/what-we-learned-from-nvidias-first-earnings-call-without-jensen-huang/), [search summary]). Oracle qualifies today. It is one notch above junk at S&P. A downgrade would move it out of Nvidia's preferred class at the moment it most needs vendor credit. That link is this report's inference; no analyst was found making it. And the "$105bn Nvidia-backed project" in the reader's notes is not an Oracle matter at all: it is Nvidia's guarantee to SB Energy for the Ohio campus leased to OpenAI (section 1), a site that does not run through Oracle. Its relevance to Oracle is that OpenAI is the tenant there and the customer here.

A figure of about 1,000bp and yields above 16% that sometimes gets attached to Oracle belongs to CoreWeave (section 3). CreditSights is reported as saying the market prices Oracle's debt as high-yield although it remains low investment grade.

The credit market's verdict is the clearest independent reading available: it did not relax after the bond sale, and it had not relaxed before Oracle spoke. The first hard evidence on vendor financing will be Oracle's 10-Q for the quarter ending 30 November, which should show it in financing cash flows or as a new liability.

### Debt maturities and refinancings in the window

There is no large bond maturity inside the window for any of the main names. Nvidia has no bond due before 2028 and an unused $25bn commercial paper programme. Oracle's next note is $2.25bn in April 2027, then a $5.1bn term loan in August 2027. CoreWeave has no bond or convertible maturing in the window but faces $2.4bn of principal in the rest of 2026, monthly amortisation starting on two 2026 facilities, and $10.6bn classified as due within twelve months of 30 September. Nebius's 364-day Citi facility runs to about October 2027. The refinancing tests are therefore CoreWeave's reported $4bn contract-backed loan, whose syndication status since 17 November is unknown, and whether SpaceX's lease closes.

Four undated items could matter more than anything in the table.

**The board committee's findings.** Wachtell and former acting US attorney Joon Kim are conducting the investigation; no mandate or timetable is public. Moody's has said it wants to see how the review concludes before considering an upgrade.

**SEC developments.** There are no scheduled SEC dates. The first formal acknowledgement, if any, would normally be in the 10-Q.

**The first deal under the $500bn platforms, the rest of OpenAI's round, and any Nscale, Lambda or Crusoe listing.** None is dated. ## One-page summary

**How the "bank" works.** Nvidia sells chips at full price and then lends its credit rating, not its cash. It holds about $114bn of equity, much of it in customers. It has promised to buy up to $32bn of capacity from neoclouds if they cannot sell it, in exchange for a share of their revenue. It has guaranteed up to $118bn of customers' obligations, of which $105bn backs OpenAI's rent on one Ohio campus and cannot become payable before fiscal 2029. And it signed non-binding memoranda with six asset managers to raise $500bn of outside money, which has not yet funded a deal. Each promise takes effect only after the customer has raised its own money and built the site.

**How big.** Guarantees are $118bn. Support to customers, on a fair middle definition, is about $150bn. The $530bn figure is every commitment Nvidia has, and more than half of it is orders to its own suppliers; on the same basis the number is now $668bn. How much revenue depends on financed customers is not disclosed. A defensible range is 3-4% on the narrowest definition, 15-30% in the middle, and above 40% on the broadest; one bank puts neoclouds at about a quarter of revenue.

**The bull case.** Nothing has been paid. Maximum guarantees are under three quarters of free cash flow. Hyperscalers are 56% of data center revenue. End demand is visible in the labs' revenue and in a $132bn backlog at CoreWeave. Kress is tightening where Lucent loosened.

**The bear case.** The guarantor was the product, and the guarantee lapses when a project is late, which is when a lender needs it. Receivables have doubled to $81bn as Nvidia gives its best customers up to a year to pay. Nvidia has $390bn of purchase commitments that assume the demand arrives. Lucent and Nortel are a poor guide to Nvidia's solvency and a fair guide to what happens to a vendor's revenue when its weakest customers lose financing: theirs fell by 57% and 62%.

**The neoclouds.** CoreWeave has $47.5bn of debt and $11bn due in 2027. Nebius plans $40-45bn of capex on $12-13bn of revenue. IREN has a stated $6bn gap. Nscale and Lambda have large contracts that depend on financing or buildings they do not yet have. The chain from closed markets to cancelled orders is documented as conditions and risk disclosures; its first links (equity window, loan terms, delivery failures) are engaged, and its later links (defaults, order cuts) are not reported. Capital is still available in size to strong credits: $44bn for SpaceX and $107bn for Anthropic in one week.

**Musk.** "Hundreds of billions" survives the arithmetic: three months of delay on $0.9-1.6tn of 2027 capex is $230-400bn. "Shortage" describes the weak borrowers. For the strong it is a price, from under 5% to over 8%.

**The SEC and the 10-Q.** An inquiry is reported, not confirmed. Its focus is unknown. The filings show an unusual number of presentation changes concentrated in one 10-Q in November 2025, including the removal of Singapore from the geographic table, and a February 2026 statement that Nvidia had entered no financing arrangements followed six months later by disclosure of payment terms of up to a year. This report's best guess, clearly labelled as such, is that disclosure about Asia ranks first and disclosure of customer financing second. The 10-Q is due on 4 December and is not late. Nvidia's history with the SEC is two settlements without intent-based fraud charges against the company, and one inquiry closed without action.

**Oracle.** "Fully funded" means Oracle will sell no more than $20bn of new securities, which it has done. The rest of the OpenAI build depends on vendor financing it has not quantified, customers prepaying or supplying chips, landlords' lenders funding $288bn of leases, and Oracle staying one notch above junk at S&P. Its credit default swaps closed at a record on the day it spoke.

**What changed in understanding.** The numbers that frighten people are mostly the wrong numbers. The exposure that matters is neither the $530bn nor the $118bn. It is the unmeasured share of revenue that existed because Nvidia's name stood behind the buyer, and the $390bn Nvidia has committed to suppliers on the assumption that it continues.

## Five things to watch

**1. Nvidia's 10-Q, due Friday 4 December.** Whether it arrives early or on the last day; whether the published numbers change; whether it names an SEC matter; whether the controls conclusion still says "effective"; and what the $9.5bn guarantee line turns out to be.

**2. Whether credit reopens for the neoclouds.** CoreWeave's reported $4bn loan, its promised $9bn of prepayments by February, and the 2027 funding plans from CoreWeave and Nebius in February. Any default, waiver or cancelled order would mark the chain moving to its later links.

**3. Oracle's quarter (9-14 December) and the 10-Q that follows.** The first sight of vendor financing as a number, the capex run-rate against the plan, and whether S&P or Moody's moves.

**4. The 2027 capex guides from Microsoft, Meta, Alphabet and Amazon (late January to early February), read alongside Nvidia's receivables.** Strong guides with DSO falling would favour the bulls. Strong guides with DSO still rising would mean Nvidia is financing more of them.

**5. What Nvidia does with the discretion it holds.** The choice of CEO; whether it exercises the option to guarantee the other 3.8 GW in Ohio (if it declines, OpenAI must find another A- rated guarantor); whether a first deal closes under the $500bn platforms and at what level of Nvidia support; and whether the $32bn of neocloud commitments shrinks again.
