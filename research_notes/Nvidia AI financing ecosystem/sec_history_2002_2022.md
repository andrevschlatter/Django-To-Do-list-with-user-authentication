## 1. Timeline: when the SEC inquiry began, the audit-committee review, the restatement, periods restated and net effect

### Takeaway
The conduct was in February-June 2000; the SEC staff raised accounting concerns with Nvidia in January 2002; Nvidia announced an internal review on 14 February 2002; the audit committee finished and the restatement was announced on 29 April 2002 (the day the CFO went on leave) and was filed in the FY2002 Form 10-K on 14 May 2002; the SEC order and the Hoberg complaint/consent were both dated 11 September 2003. The restatement was small and **cumulatively increased** three-year net income by about $1.3 million (FY2000 up, FY2001 down), with annual revenue unchanged for FY2000 and FY2001.

## 2. What was misstated, and the story of the approximately $3.3 million supplier transaction

### Takeaway
The restatement's categories were mostly inter-period timing items (manufacturing costs, inventory write-downs, accruals, deferred revenue/customer programs). The enforcement cases rest on one transaction: in Q1 FY2001 (quarter ended 30 April 2000) an unnamed chip manufacturing/packaging supplier gave Nvidia about $3.3 million of credits in exchange for Nvidia's promise to repay through higher prices in August 2000; the CFO had the deal papered as two separate letters, Nvidia booked only the credit, and auditors were shown only the credit letter. That let the quarter beat consensus on gross margin and earnings; gross profit was overstated 6.4%, net income 15.3% and diluted EPS 14.8%.

### Cited Findings

**Categories of restatement adjustments**
- FY2000 and FY2001 (final, per 10-K): revenue adjustments "limited to intra-year changes" (deferred revenue in FY2001; accruals for estimated product returns in FY2000); cost of revenues: mainly inter-year changes in recording certain manufacturing costs plus timing of inventory write-downs; operating expenses: mainly inter-year timing of accruals (†, paraphrase). FY2002: intra-year deferred revenue and customer-program accrual changes; manufacturing cost and inventory write-down timing; accrual timing. [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [NVIDIA Form 10-K FY2002, filed 14 May 2002](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000101287002002262/d10k.htm)
- Nvidia's 1 May 2002 Reg FD 8-K grouped the adjustments into four types: (1) mistakes found in the review (example: an expense booked twice); (2) accruals where "we concluded that there was not sufficient current evidence to support an estimate" (†); (3) earlier accounting judgments later found incorrect (the filing does not say whether made in good or bad faith, per the fetch summary); (4) previously waived audit adjustments from quarterly reviews and annual audits. No dollar amounts by category. [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [NVIDIA Form 8-K, 1 May 2002](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000101287002002054/d8k.htm)
- Balance-sheet effect at 28 Jan 2001 was tiny: inventory 90,380 restated vs 89,905; retained earnings 130,016 vs 128,623. [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [NVIDIA Form 10-K FY2002](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000101287002002262/d10k.htm)

**The supplier transaction (all quotes in this block are verbatim from the raw complaint text unless marked †)**
- Motive and quarter: "In early 2000, Nvidia's internal forecasting identified that the Company was lagging behind expectations for its financial results for the quarter ended April 30, 2000, the first fiscal quarter of Nvidia's fiscal year 2001. In order to address this anticipated shortfall, Nvidia's senior management immediately focused the Company on reducing costs for the quarter in order to bolster gross margins and net income." [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Complaint, para 11](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf)
- Supplier identity: **not named** in either document. The complaint describes "the companies that manufactured and packaged Nvidia's graphics chips" and calls the counterparty "Nvidia's supplier". [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Complaint, para 12](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf)
- Sizing the ask to the shortfall: Nvidia employees "communicated with Hoberg and other senior executives, who ensured that the amount of cost savings sought from Nvidia's supplier was directly correlated to the amount required to address the forecasted income, earnings per share and gross margin shortfalls for the quarter. Hoberg directed that the amount of cost savings from Nvidia's supplier needed to be approximately $3 million." [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Complaint, para 13](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf)
- What the supplier offered and what it wanted back: on 30 March 2000 the supplier's written proposal "offered to grant the cost reductions requested by Nvidia by providing credits for costs of materials owed by Nvidia to Nvidia's supplier in the current quarter. In return, Nvidia would reimburse the credits by agreeing to pay higher prices for other goods ordered from Nvidia's supplier later in the year. The proposal called this a 'cost transfer.'" The proposal stated the supplier would "agree to credit Nvidia $3M for cost transfer from Q1 2000 to August 2000 . . . [and that] Nvidia [would] agree to re-pay the $3M back to [Nvidia's supplier] in August 2000 by means of a . . . total price adjustment [of] $3,037,500." [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Complaint, para 14](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf)
- The CFO's email: "Responding by email, Hoberg wrote 'can not sign this or have this in print. Will wipe out the credit in Q1. [N]eed to arrange this separately and trust us to abide by it.'" [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Complaint, para 15](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf). The company order quotes the same email and attributes it to "Nvidia's CFO" without naming her (†). — [SEC Order 34-48480](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-48480.htm)
- How it should have been accounted for: "Under GAAP, and specifically Statement of Financial Accounting Standards No. 5, paragraph 8, Nvidia's explicit agreement to repay the costs to its supplier created a liability that Nvidia was required to record in its accounting records. Thus, under GAAP, the recording of the liability would offset the credits." [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Complaint, para 16](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf)
- Splitting the paper: "Hoberg directed that the deal be split into two separate agreements. In the first agreement, signed on April 3, 2000 by an Nvidia employee under the supervision of Hoberg, Nvidia agreed to pay increased prices to the supplier in August 2000 in the total amount of $3.3 million. In the second agreement, signed by Nvidia's supplier on April 4, 2000, the supplier granted credits and cost waivers to Nvidia in the amount of $3.3 million for the April 30, 2000 quarter." [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Complaint, para 17](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf). The company order gives the 3 April figure as "$3,299,000" and adds "Neither agreement makes reference to the other." (†) — [SEC Order 34-48480](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-48480.htm)
- How it was actually booked: "At Hoberg's direction, at the close of Nvidia's fiscal quarter ended April 30, 2000, Nvidia recorded on its books the $3.3 million in credits granted by the April 4th agreement, but did not make any offsetting entry for the obligation to pay higher prices in August 2000." [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Complaint, para 18](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf)
- The auditors: "In early May 2000, Nvidia's independent auditors conducted a quarterly review of Nvidia's financial records. As Hoberg was aware, Nvidia presented the April 4th agreement as support for reducing its quarterly costs by $3.3 million. However, Hoberg did not inform the auditors of Nvidia's April 3rd agreement to pay higher prices on future purchases from its supplier." [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Complaint, para 19](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf)
- Effect and expectations: "Hoberg caused Nvidia to overstate its gross profit and income before taxes by $3.3 million for the quarter ended April 30, 2000." "On May 16, 2000, Nvidia issued a press release reporting record financial results for the quarter ended April 30, 2000. The reported results exceeded analysts' expectations for gross margin and net income. On June 14, 2000, Nvidia included its financial results for the April 30, 2000 quarter in a Form 10-Q filed with the Commission, which Hoberg signed." [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Complaint, paras 20-21](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf)
- Size relative to the quarter: "Nvidia's originally reported results for the April 30, 2000 quarter overstated its gross profit by 6.4% and its net income by 15.3%." [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Complaint, para 3](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf). The company order adds diluted EPS: "Nvidia's originally reported gross profit was overstated by 6.4%, net income by 15.3%, and diluted earnings per share by 14.8%." (†) — [SEC Order 34-48480](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-48480.htm)
- The company order says the conduct "allowed the Company to exceed the consensus analysts' estimates for earnings, income, and product gross margin" and that "The reported results exceeded consensus external forecasts for revenue, income, earnings per share, and gross margin." (†) Neither document gives the consensus figures. [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Order 34-48480](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-48480.htm)
- As originally reported for the quarter ended 30 Apr 2000: revenue $148,483K; cost of revenue $92,975K; gross profit $55,508K (37.4% of revenue); net income $18,287K; diluted EPS $0.47 (pre-split share count 38,889K); signed by "Christine B. Hoberg, Chief Financial Officer (Principal Financial and Accounting Officer)" on 14 June 2000 (†). [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [NVIDIA Form 10-Q for quarter ended 30 Apr 2000, NVIDIA/EDGAR, filed 14 Jun 2000](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000101287000003358/0001012870-00-003358-0001.txt)
- A twist recorded only in the company order, footnote 1: "The Company, through its audit committee, determined that the increased prices reflected in a April 3, 2000 letter agreement (described below) were not, in fact, paid in the third quarter of fiscal year 2001 or subsequently. As a result, the restatement did not include adjustments reflecting any such payments." (†) [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Order 34-48480](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-48480.htm)
- Reversal: "On April 29, 2002, following the completion of an internal investigation by its audit committee, Nvidia restated its financial results for the quarter ended April 30, 2000. As part of the restatement, the previously recognized $3.3 million in cost reductions from Nvidia's supplier was reversed." [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Complaint, para 22](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf)

### Inferences
- Implied restated Q1 FY2001 figures (my arithmetic from the originally reported numbers and the SEC's percentages, not a quoted source): gross profit about $52.2 million (reported $55.5 million; difference about $3.3 million), gross margin about 35.1% vs 37.4% reported, net income about $15.9 million (reported $18.3 million; difference about $2.4 million), diluted EPS about $0.41 vs $0.47 (pre-split). The net income gap (about $2.4 million) slightly exceeds $3.3 million after tax at Nvidia's 32% rate (about $2.2 million), consistent with the order's statement that the overstatement stemmed "primarily" from this one transaction.
- The transaction was economically a one-quarter loan of margin from a supplier: a cost shifted from Q1 to Q3 of the same fiscal year. That is why the annual effect is small while the quarterly effect was material. The footnote that the higher prices were never actually paid means the supplier credit was never in fact repaid, but that does not cure the Q1 accounting, which had to reflect the obligation that existed at the time.
- "Fiscal 2001" Q1 is calendar February-April 2000. Press references to "2000" vs "fiscal 2001" describe the same quarter.

### Gaps
- The supplier's name is not in the SEC documents; I found no primary source naming it.
- Consensus EPS/gross-margin numbers for the quarter are not given in the SEC documents and I could not read contemporaneous press.
- The Accountancy Age figure of "$3.6m" (vs the SEC's $3.3 million) could not be reconciled because the article could not be opened [UNVERIFIED].

## 3. Christine B. Hoberg: role, what was alleged, provisions charged, resolution

### Takeaway
Hoberg (CFO December 1998 to April 2002, resigned 31 July 2002, a California CPA) was the only individual charged. The SEC sued her in federal court for "financial reporting fraud": Section 10(b)/Rule 10b-5, plus Section 13(b)(5) and Rules 13b2-1 and 13b2-2. It was settled on filing: without admitting or denying, she consented to a permanent injunction, a five-year officer-and-director bar, and payment of $671,694.99 ($596,694.99 disgorgement plus prejudgment interest, and a $75,000 civil penalty). She was **not** charged under Securities Act Section 17(a) or with aiding and abetting Section 13(a).

### Cited Findings
- Position and tenure: "Christine B. Hoberg, age 48, resides in Los Altos, California. She was the Chief Financial Officer of Nvidia from December 1998 until April 2002 when she was placed on administrative leave. Hoberg resigned her employment on July 31, 2002. Hoberg is a Certified Public Accountant licensed by the State of California. As Chief Financial Officer of Nvidia, Hoberg had overall responsibility for the company's financial books and records, financial reporting and fiscal management." [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Complaint, para 9](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf)
- Characterisation: "This matter concerns fraudulent financial reporting by Christine B. Hoberg, former Chief Financial Officer of Nvidia Corporation". [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Complaint, para 1](https://www.sec.gov/files/Complaint03-cv-04135Hoberg.pdf)
- State of mind alleged: First claim: Hoberg "intentionally or recklessly: (a) employed devices, schemes, or artifices to defraud; (b) made untrue statements of material facts or omitted to state material facts ...; and (c) engaged in acts, practices, or courses of business which operated or would operate as a fraud or deceit". ## 4. What exactly the SEC charged the COMPANY with, and whether "fraud" is the right word

### Takeaway
**"Fraud" is not an accurate description of the company's 2003 settlement.** The SEC's settled administrative order found that Nvidia violated only the periodic-reporting, books-and-records and internal-accounting-controls provisions of the Exchange Act (Sections 13(a), 13(b)(2)(A), 13(b)(2)(B); Rules 12b-20 and 13a-13). No antifraud provision (Section 10(b)/Rule 10b-5 or Securities Act Section 17(a)) was charged or found against the company, no penalty was imposed, the only sanction was a cease-and-desist order, and Nvidia neither admitted nor denied the findings. The fraud charge was against the former CFO individually.

### Cited Findings
- Form of action: administrative cease-and-desist proceeding under Exchange Act Section 21C, instituted and settled simultaneously. Caption: "ORDER INSTITUTING CEASE-AND-DESIST PROCEEDINGS, MAKING FINDINGS, AND IMPOSING A CEASE-AND-DESIST ORDER PURSUANT TO SECTION 21C OF THE SECURITIES EXCHANGE ACT OF 1934"; "Release No. 48480 / September 11, 2003"; Accounting and Auditing Enforcement "Release No. 1859 / September 11, 2003"; Administrative Proceeding "File No. 3-11255" (†). [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Order 34-48480, SEC, 11 Sep 2003](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-48480.htm) (current URL form: https://www.sec.gov/enforcement-litigation/administrative-proceedings/34-48480)
- No admission: Nvidia consented "without admitting or denying the findings herein," except as to jurisdiction (†). [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Order 34-48480](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-48480.htm)
- What the SEC found the company did: "Nvidia issued the restatement in part because it had inflated its quarterly results by entering into an agreement with a supplier that allowed Nvidia to reduce its costs by more than $3 million during the April 30, 2000 quarter, in exchange for Nvidia's agreement to repay that amount through higher prices on other products in a future period. By recording the cost reduction, but not the corresponding agreement to pay higher prices in the future, Nvidia inflated its results for the quarter ended April 30, 2000 and allowed the Company to exceed the consensus analysts' estimates for earnings, income, and product gross margin." (†, assembled from consecutive fragments) [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Order 34-48480](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-48480.htm)
- Strong language is nonetheless in the company order: "Nvidia engaged in accounting misconduct intended to boost the Company's financial results." and "the press release and the Form 10-Q were false and misleading when made." (†) [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Order 34-48480](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-48480.htm)
- Legal conclusions: "As a result, Nvidia's Form 10-Q for the period ended April 30, 2000, which was filed with the Commission and disseminated to investors, as well as Nvidia's press release on May 16, 2000, were materially false and misleading. In addition, the Company failed to maintain books, records and accounts that, in reasonable detail, accurately and fairly reflected its transactions and dispositions of assets and failed to maintain a system of internal accounting controls sufficient to permit the preparation of financial statements in conformity with GAAP." "Based on the foregoing, the Commission finds that the Company violated Sections 13(a), 13(b)(2)(A) and 13(b)(2)(B) of the Exchange Act and Rules 12b-20 and 13a-13 thereunder." (†) [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Order 34-48480](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-48480.htm)
- Antifraud provisions: the fetch tool reports that the words "fraud", "10(b)", "17(a)" and "scienter" do not appear anywhere in the order, and that the order makes no explicit state-of-mind finding about any individual (the CFO is referred to by title only, never by name) (†). [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Order 34-48480](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-48480.htm)
- Sanction: "Respondent Nvidia shall cease and desist from committing or causing any violation and any future violation of Sections 13(a), 13(b)(2)(A) and 13(b)(2)(B) of the Exchange Act, and Rules 12b-20 and 13a-13 thereunder." No civil penalty, disgorgement, monitor or undertaking is ordered (†). [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Order 34-48480](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-48480.htm)
- Credit for cooperation/remediation: "In determining to accept the Offer, the Commission considered remedial acts promptly undertaken by the Respondent and cooperation afforded the Commission staff, after the staff had raised issues concerning Nvidia's books and records." (†) [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Order 34-48480](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-48480.htm)
- Nvidia's own characterisation: NVIDIA, "neither admitting nor denying any wrongdoing," agreed to an administrative cease-and-desist order concerning future violations of "non-fraud financial reporting, books and records, and internal control provisions" of the federal securities laws; "no penalty or fine has been assessed" (†). [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [NVIDIA press release, 11 Sep 2003 (Form 8-K filed 12 Sep 2003)](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001045810/000089109203002448/e15681ex99-1.txt). Repeated in [NVIDIA Form 10-Q filed 21 Nov 2003](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581003000010/q30410q.htm): "without admitting or denying liability or wrongdoing"; "There is no requirement to pay any fines or penalties." (†)
- Remediation steps on record: the audit committee's recommendations included creating a general counsel position, an internal audit function and financial-reporting training (†, paraphrase); CEO Jen-Hsun Huang, 29 Apr 2002: "The Audit Committee conducted a complete and thorough review of all matters raised over the last three fiscal years." "In addition, we have the necessary systems and staff today to ensure the integrity of our financial reporting." (†) [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [NVIDIA 2002 proxy, Audit Committee report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001045810/000101287002002524/ddef14a.htm); [NVIDIA press release, 29 Apr 2002](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000095016802001116/dex991.htm)

### Inferences (precise wording recommendations)
- Accurate: "In September 2003 Nvidia settled, without admitting or denying the findings, an SEC administrative order finding that it had violated the reporting, books-and-records and internal-controls provisions of the Exchange Act by filing a materially false and misleading Form 10-Q for the quarter ended April 30, 2000. The SEC imposed a cease-and-desist order and no financial penalty, citing the company's prompt remediation and cooperation. Separately, the SEC charged the company's former CFO with financial reporting fraud; she settled without admitting or denying."
- Inaccurate or overstated: "Nvidia settled SEC fraud charges in 2003"; "Nvidia was fined"; "Nvidia admitted accounting fraud". Also inaccurate in the other direction: "the SEC found only technical bookkeeping errors" — the order says the 10-Q and press release were "materially false and misleading" and describes "accounting misconduct intended to boost the Company's financial results".
- Sections 13(a), 13(b)(2)(A) and 13(b)(2)(B) are strict-liability/non-scienter provisions. The SEC chose not to impute the CFO's alleged scienter to the company in a 10(b) charge. A fair one-line gloss: "a non-fraud settlement by the company arising from conduct the SEC called fraudulent when charging the individual responsible."
- It is defensible to say the SEC "alleged a fraud *at* Nvidia by its CFO" (the litigation release's own words are "financial reporting fraud charges against Christine B. Hoberg"); it is not defensible to say the SEC charged *Nvidia* with fraud.

### Gaps
- The full verbatim text of the 2003 order could not be captured as raw text (sec.gov blocked `curl`; the fetch tool returns only short fragments). All fragments quoted above should be re-checked against the live page before publication in quotation marks.
- I found no SEC press release (as distinct from the litigation release and administrative order) for the 2003 actions.

## 6. The May 2022 crypto-mining disclosure order

### Takeaway
On 6 May 2022 the SEC issued a settled cease-and-desist order finding that Nvidia's Forms 10-Q for the second and third quarters of fiscal 2018 failed to disclose that crypto-mining was a significant factor in gaming revenue growth, in violation of Securities Act Sections 17(a)(2) and (3), Exchange Act Section 13(a) and Rules 12b-20 and 13a-13, and the disclosure-controls rule 13a-15(a). Nvidia paid a $5.5 million penalty without admitting or denying. The order itself states the 17(a)(2)/(3) violations do not require scienter and may rest on negligence, so "fraud" is again imprecise. No individual was charged in the order. The parallel shareholder class action survived the Supreme Court and a class was certified on 25 March 2026; no settlement was disclosed as of Nvidia's 26 August 2026 Form 10-Q.

### Cited Findings (all quotes in this block are verbatim from the raw order text unless marked †)
- Identification: Securities Act Release No. 11060; Exchange Act Release No. 94859; 6 May 2022; Administrative Proceeding File No. 3-20844; "ORDER INSTITUTING CEASE-AND-DESIST PROCEEDINGS PURSUANT TO SECTION 8A OF THE SECURITIES ACT OF 1933 AND SECTION 21C OF THE SECURITIES EXCHANGE ACT OF 1934, MAKING FINDINGS, AND IMPOSING A CEASE-AND-DESIST ORDER". [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Order, *In the Matter of NVIDIA Corporation*, SEC, 6 May 2022](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2022/33-11060.pdf)
- No admission: "without admitting or denying the findings herein, except as to the Commission's jurisdiction over it and the subject matter of these proceedings, which are admitted". [FILING/ON-RECORD] — [SEC Order 33-11060, Section II](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2022/33-11060.pdf)

NVIDIA had information indicating that cryptomining was a significant factor in the year-over-year growth in revenue from the sale of GPUs that NVIDIA designed and marketed for gaming. ### Inferences

Sections 17(a)(2) and (3) sit in the Securities Act's antifraud section, and 17(a)(3)'s statutory text speaks of conduct that "operates or would operate as a fraud or deceit", so some lawyers loosely call them "negligence-based antifraud provisions". But the order expressly says they require no scienter, the SEC's own headline is "Inadequate Disclosures", and no intentional or reckless misconduct is found. Accurate: "settled, without admitting or denying, negligence-based disclosure charges and a disclosure-controls charge; paid a $5.5 million penalty." Inaccurate: "settled securities fraud charges" (without the negligence qualifier) or "was found to have defrauded investors".
- The 2022 order differs in kind from 2003: 2003 was a books-and-records/accounting case (a number was wrong and was restated); 2022 was an MD&A narrative-disclosure case (numbers were right; the explanation of what drove them was incomplete). 2022 also carried a money penalty and a Securities Act charge, which 2003 did not.

### Inferences (sober relevance assessment)
- **Where the history is genuinely relevant.**
  1. *Charging pattern.* In both prior cases the SEC charged the company only under non-scienter provisions (2003: Sections 13(a), 13(b)(2)(A), 13(b)(2)(B); 2022: Sections 17(a)(2)-(3), 13(a), Rule 13a-15(a)) and reserved the scienter-based fraud charge for an individual (2003 only). The GIR headline's prediction of "internal controls, disclosures" as the likely focus is consistent with that pattern, but it is a lawyer's prediction, not a fact about the current inquiry.
  2. *Recidivism as an SEC factor.* A company with two prior cease-and-desist orders, the second of which (2022) orders it to cease and desist from future violations of Section 13(a) and Rules 12b-20, 13a-13 and 13a-15, is a repeat respondent on periodic-reporting provisions. Prior orders are a recognised aggravating consideration when the SEC weighs penalties and credit for cooperation. The 2003 order's no-penalty outcome rested expressly on "remedial acts promptly undertaken" and "cooperation"; that leniency is harder to obtain a third time.
  3. *Continuity.* The CEO was the same person across 2000-2003, 2006, 2017-2022 and until 11 Nov 2026. The current interim CEO has been CFO since 2013 and so presided over the finance function during the period covered by the 2022 order. PwC has audited continuously since 2004 (covering 2017-18 but not 2000-2002). So the 2022 matter involves the same CEO, CFO and auditor as the present; the 2002-03 matter shares only the CEO (and long-serving directors such as Harvey Jones, on the board since 1993).
  4. *Analogy of substance.* The 2022 order was about failing to explain the *quality and sustainability* of a demand driver (crypto-miners buying gaming GPUs) that investors were explicitly asking about. If the present questions concern how much AI-infrastructure revenue is supported by Nvidia's own financing, guarantees, investments or extended terms, the MD&A theory the SEC used in 2022 (Item 303 "known trends"; "likelihood that past performance is indicative of future performance") is the closest precedent. This is an analytical parallel, not something any source I read asserts.
- **Where it is only colour.**
  1. The 2000 conduct is 26 years old, involved roughly $3.3 million in one quarter, a different CFO (who left in 2002), a different auditor, a company with under $1 billion of revenue, and a pre-Sarbanes-Oxley control environment. The restatement *raised* cumulative income. It says little about controls in 2026.
  2. Neither prior order found scienter by the company, and neither named Huang or Kress. Using the history to imply personal culpability of current or recently departed management would go beyond the record.
  3. The 2022 penalty ($5.5 million) was immaterial to Nvidia then and is trivially small against its current scale; headlines that pair "$5.5M" with the current probe convey precedent, not magnitude.
  4. The 2006-07 options inquiry shows that an SEC inquiry at this company, even alongside a nine-figure restatement, has ended with no action.
- **Balance.** The fair framing is: "Nvidia has twice before settled SEC disclosure or accounting cases without admitting wrongdoing, in neither case on scienter-based fraud charges against the company; the more recent, in 2022, concerned inadequate disclosure of what was driving revenue growth and occurred under the same CEO, CFO and auditor." Calling the company a prior "fraud" settler is inaccurate; ignoring that it is a repeat respondent on reporting provisions is also incomplete.
- The combination on the record in November 2026 (immediate CEO resignation with no stated reason, an independent board committee investigation of undisclosed subject, and a 10-Q not filed with earnings) is far more probative of the seriousness of the present situation than anything in the 2003 or 2022 history. The filings do not connect these three facts to each other or to an SEC inquiry; any such connection is inference.
