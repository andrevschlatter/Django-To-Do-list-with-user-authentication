# D1 Capital Partners (Dan Sundheim) and Coatue Management (Philippe Laffont): firm-level economics 2016–2025 and the implied pool for non-founder investment staff

Research-method note for the report writer: in this session direct page fetches were blocked by the network proxy for essentially every relevant domain (institutionalinvestor.com, forbes.com, bloomberg.com, sec.gov / adviserinfo.sec.gov, wikipedia, whalewisdom, hedgeweek, yahoo, smartasset, wallstreetoasis, etc.), and the shared web-search budget was exhausted before every gap could be closed. Everything below therefore comes from search-engine result snippets/summaries of the cited pages, not from full-text reads of the underlying articles or of the primary SEC Form ADV filings. Form ADV Item 5.B headcounts and Schedule A ownership for the current filings could NOT be read directly and are listed as gaps where no secondary source reported them.

Conventions: "REPORTED" = figure attributed to a named source. "ESTIMATE/ASSUMPTION" = my own modelling input. All USD.

## Key Question 1: What did Dan Sundheim and Philippe Laffont earn each year 2016–2025 per II Rich List / Forbes / Bloomberg, and what are the methodology caveats?

### Takeaway
Laffont has an almost complete Institutional Investor (II) Rich List series ($550M for 2017; off the list for 2018; $190M for 2019; $1.6B for 2020; $800M for 2021; off the list for 2022; $1.4B for 2023; $1.9B for 2024; $1.8B for 2025). Sundheim's series is patchier ($225M "Second Team" for 2019; ~$1.1B for 2020 per Bloomberg's list; ~$1.0B for 2021; $375M for 2024; $1.4B for 2025; nothing found for 2018, 2022 or 2023). Both lists count the manager's share of firm fees PLUS gains on the manager's own capital in the funds, so they are not fee income and cannot be subtracted from fee revenue without first stripping out own-capital gains.

### Cited Findings

**Methodology caveats**
- II's Rich List "counts an individual's gains on their own capital invested in their funds plus their share of the fees" — [II 25th Rich List](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/hedge-funds/rich-list-institutional-investors-25th-annual-ranking-highest-earning-hedge); the 22nd list describes the ranking as "a mix of fee income and returns on managers' own capital" — [II 22nd Rich List](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bstpd4t08pc8xwj4we80/corner-office/the-rich-list-the-22nd-annual-ranking-of-the-highest-earning-hedge-fund-managers).
- II's ranking is a one-year snapshot: "a manager may have lost a large amount of personal capital during a down year, [but] he may still see that capital benefit when a fund goes up" — [Forbes 400 2021 hedge fund coverage](https://www.forbes.com/sites/sergeiklebnikov/2021/10/05/richest-hedge-fund-managers-forbes-400-list-2021/).
- Forbes net-worth methodology "weighs fund returns and the fee and ownership structure of each firm" and adds other assets (real estate, aircraft, art); it measures net worth, not annual earnings — [Forbes 400 2021 hedge fund coverage](https://www.forbes.com/sites/sergeiklebnikov/2021/10/05/richest-hedge-fund-managers-forbes-400-list-2021/).
- Bloomberg's and II's lists disagree for the same year: for 2020, Bloomberg credited Chase Coleman with $3B vs II's $2.5B, and Laffont with $1.7B (Bloomberg) vs $1.6B (II) — [Bloomberg 2020 list, secondary summary](https://www.business-powerhouse.com/best-paid-hedge-fund-managers-2020); [Forbes 400 2021 coverage](https://www.forbes.com/sites/sergeiklebnikov/2021/10/05/richest-hedge-fund-managers-forbes-400-list-2021/).
- Rich List qualifying minimums (useful for bounding "off-list" years): $130M for 2016 and $225M for 2017 — [II 17th Rich List via search summary](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2bsxdilrx2cvaechjb400/john-overdeck-david-siegel); $375M for 2024 ("highest minimum in the ranking's 24-year history") — [II 24th Rich List](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/2ekchq8rll8mbbn2glq80/hedge-funds/the-rich-list-the-24th-annual-ranking-of-the-highest-earning-hedge-fund-managers); $470M for 2025 — [II 25th Rich List / PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/institutional-investor-unveils-25th-annual-rich-list-302726943.html).

### Inferences
- Laffont's Rich List figures track the hedge fund's HWM status closely (off-list in 2018 and 2022; back on with $1.4B in 2023 even though the fund was still underwater — which implies a large part of the 2023 figure was gains on his own capital and/or private-platform fees rather than hedge-fund incentive fees).
- Sundheim's 2024 figure ($375M, exactly the cut-off) is consistent with D1 regaining its HWM only late in 2024 (little crystallised incentive) plus a modest gain on his own capital; his 2025 figure ($1.4B) is consistent with a full incentive year on the public book plus accrued/unrealised gains on privates (SpaceX) and his own capital.
- Given the own-capital component, the founder's fee share is materially lower than the headline: e.g., if Sundheim had ~$1.0–1.5B in the fund at start-2025, a ~34% return alone is ~$0.35–0.5B of the $1.4B.

### Gaps
- Sundheim: no figure found for 2018, 2022, 2023; II's own 20th-list (2020) number for him was not retrievable (only Bloomberg's ~$1.1B).
- Laffont: no figure found for 2016; 2018 and 2022 are "off list" with no dollar estimate published.
- Bloomberg Billionaires Index year-by-year net worth for either man was not retrievable; Forbes' net-worth-over-time charts were not readable.
- Whether II nets out firm operating expenses and staff compensation before attributing a "share of fees" was not confirmed from the snippets.

## Key Question 2: What were AUM, net returns, and fee terms each year for D1's flagship (public/private hybrid) and for Coatue's hedge fund plus its venture/growth funds?

### Takeaway
D1 grew from >$5B at launch (July 2018) to ~$20B (Jan 2021), ~$24B (2022), $26.1B regulatory AUM (Mar 2024), $31.1B at end-2025 (~two-thirds private) and $40.2B regulatory AUM (Mar 2026); returns were +36.8% (2019), +54–61% (2020), roughly flat-to-mixed by share class (2021), −30.5 to −34% (2022), ~+0.8% (2023), publics +44%/privates +3.7% (2024), +33.7% largest class (2025). D1's disclosed fee framework is a 1–2% management fee plus a performance fee on net capital appreciation, with privates held in side-pocket sleeves and share classes with differing private exposure. Coatue's firm-wide AUM went from ~$9–16B (2017–18) to $70B+ (Q1 2022), $50.5B (Mar 2023), $58.6B (Mar 2024), $67B (Sep 2025) and ~$70B (Mar 2026), with the flagship hedge fund only ~$14B and the rest in growth/venture/structured vehicles; hedge-fund returns were +25% (2017), ~flat/−1.2% (2018), +65% (2020), +5–7% (2021), −19% (2022), +21.5% (2023), +18.7% (2024), +13.9% (2025). Coatue's brochure discloses only ranges: fixed fee 0–2.5% p.a. and performance compensation 0–33% with loss carry-forward.

**D1 Capital — fee structure and vehicle terms**
- Management fees "in a band of 1% to 2% per year", charged to the funds, "pegged to the net value of each share class"; performance compensation "calculated separately for each investor's capital account, at the end of each fiscal year," "based on net capital appreciation," taken "from every client"; co-investment vehicles "all fees are subject to negotiation"; funds also bear "registration, maintenance, tax, and regulatory costs" — [SmartAsset D1 review (summarising ADV brochure)](https://smartasset.com/financial-advisor/d1-capital-partners-review).

## Key Question 5: What is the implied comp pool per investment professional, and how does it compare with the claim (Year-1 $0.3–0.6M; years 1–5 avg $0.5–1.5M; years 1–10 avg $1.5–4M; tenure 2–5 years)?

### Takeaway
A top-down fee model (ESTIMATE) says D1 generates roughly $300–400M/yr of management fees alone at $20–27B AUM (or $200–270M at a 1% rate), plus $0.6–1.8B of incentive fees in strong years (2019, 2020, 2025) and essentially nothing in 2022–mid-2024; after a ~$40–50M overhead and the founder's reported take (net of his own-capital gains), the arithmetic leaves a few hundred million dollars for ~35–38 investment professionals in good years (≈$8–38M per head, an upper bound that is clearly not all paid out) and the management fee alone could fund ~$1–3M per investment professional even in zero-incentive years. For Coatue, the hedge fund alone cannot explain Laffont's $1.4–1.9B figures for 2023–25 (hedge-fund gross fees are modelled at only $0.2–0.75B), so the private platform's management fees (~$300–375M/yr on ~$20–25B committed) plus accrued growth-fund carry and Laffont's own-capital gains must be doing the work; the resulting staff pool is not derivable from the founder figure and is better bounded by headcount × anecdotal pay. Both firms' economics are comfortably consistent with the claimed pay ladder; the binding constraint on realised pay is HWM status (2022–24 for both), not the size of the pool.

### Inferences (how to read the arithmetic)
- D1, strong years (2019, 2020, 2025): the mechanical residual ($0.46–1.2B, i.e., $12–38M per investment professional) is far above any plausible analyst pay and should be read as an UPPER BOUND on the staff pool, not a payout. The excess is explained by some combination of (i) Rich List underestimation of Sundheim (the 2019 figure of $225M against modelled gross fees of ~$750M is the clearest case), (ii) founders'-class or discounted fee terms on launch capital, (iii) incentive accrued on side-pocket marks but not crystallised, and (iv) retained earnings accruing to the founder-owner. Any of these means the true staff pool is a fraction of the residual.
- D1, weak years (2021, 2022–23): the residual goes negative or collapses to "management fee minus overhead" (~$250M/yr base, ~$150M low case). Even that floor is ~$4–7M per investment professional, so D1's management fee alone could fund a Year-1 $0.3–0.6M / seasoned $1–2M ladder for ~37 people (~$40–60M/yr total) with hundreds of millions to spare for the founder; the 2022–mid-2024 period without incentive fees is the main reason bonuses would have been compressed, not pool exhaustion.
- Coatue: the hedge-fund-only model produces negative residuals in 2021 and 2023–25 because Laffont's reported earnings exceed modelled hedge-fund gross fees. This is diagnostic: a large part of his 2023–25 figures must be (a) management fees and accrued carry from the ~$20–25B private platform, (b) gains on his own capital in growth/venture funds (the newer growth funds return >45% annually per internal data), and/or (c) a higher hedge-fund fee rate than 1.5/20 (the brochure allows up to 2.5%/33%). The staff pool at Coatue therefore cannot be backed out from the founder figure; a bottom-up bound is more useful: ~110–120 investment professionals × $0.5–1.5M average (claimed ladder) = $55–180M/yr, versus modelled management fees alone of $500–600M/yr (2023–25) minus ~$150–215M overhead = $300–450M before any incentive fee — i.e., the claimed ladder is affordable roughly 2–8× over.
- Comparison with the claim (Year-1 $0.3–0.6M; years 1–5 avg $0.5–1.5M; years 1–10 avg $1.5–4M; tenure 2–5 years):
  - Affordability: for both firms, management-fee revenue alone (even in HWM-underwater years) exceeds the total cost of the claimed ladder by a wide margin; incentive-fee years add $0.3–2B of additional pool. The claim is economically conservative relative to the fee pool.
  - Level: anonymous reports of $600k–$1M first-year pay at the largest Tiger Cubs (Coatue, Viking, Lone Pine, Maverick) and $1–2M for seasoned analysts (up to $3–5M in strong years) sit at or slightly above the top of the claimed ranges; the WSO claim that analyst comp is "capped at the low and high-ends" supports the idea of a bounded ladder rather than open-ended profit participation.
  - Variance: the HWM cycle (D1: no incentive 2022–mid-2024; Coatue: 2018, 2022–23) implies realised multi-year averages for an analyst who joined in 2021–22 would be at the bottom of the claimed ranges, while a 2019–20 or 2025 cohort would be at or above the top.
  - Tenure: the documented Coatue partner exodus (2022–25), a "2-year analyst stint" culture per forum posts, and D1's 2023–24 team turnover are consistent with the 2–5-year average tenure claim; II's note that D1 lateral-hired PMs rarely work out suggests D1's ladder is built on PE-trained juniors rather than a stable senior partner class.
- Distribution of the pool is skewed: with no evidence of a broad equity-partner class at either firm, and the founder owning the management company, the residual beyond what is needed to pay the ladder is the founder's (which is consistent with Forbes net worth of $4.5B for Sundheim and $9.5B for Laffont in Oct 2026).

### Gaps
- No audited bonus-pool, comp-ratio, or management-company P&L exists in the public domain for either firm; the residuals above are therefore model outputs, not observations.
- Missing inputs that most affect the estimate: exact D1 incentive rate/HWM mechanics on side-pockets; Coatue hedge-fund and growth-fund fee rates and carry crystallisation; Coatue Form ADV 5.B investment headcount; the founders' actual own-capital balances (the own-capital adjustment is the single largest swing factor in converting Rich List figures into fee share).
- Years with no founder figure (Sundheim 2018, 2022, 2023; Laffont 2016, 2018, 2022) can only be bounded by the list minimums ($130M for 2016; $225M for 2017; $375M for 2024; $470M for 2025) and by the "management fee minus overhead" floor.
