# Argentina Oil & Gas: Vaca Muerta Research Note

As of November 11, 2026. Prepared ahead of the November 16-20, 2026 investor trip to Buenos Aires and Neuquén. The shared version with the two exhibits is at https://claude.ai/artifact/VDYJ6Qp1BV5a5iuT5fw6Fa

## Introduction

Argentina is the only country outside North America where shale oil works at scale. Vaca Muerta, a formation in northern Patagonia about the size of Belgium, now produces seven of every ten Argentine barrels and has pushed national output to a record of nearly one million barrels a day, double the level of 2017. The rock is not the constraint: its best wells out-produce good Permian wells foot for foot, though they cost about 30% more to drill. What held the play back sat above ground, in full pipelines, capital controls that trapped profits and governments that set prices. All three are changing at once. A new oil export pipeline to the Atlantic starts up in early 2027, the main gas pipeline grows by two-thirds a few weeks later and large-scale LNG exports begin by year-end. The partners in the far larger Argentina LNG project are due to commit on November 26, the week after this trip. President Milei's investment regime, RIGI, offers large projects thirty years of tax and currency stability, and foreign companies can again send new profits home, though older earnings stay trapped. YPF, the state-controlled champion, operates half the basin's oil, and most global majors have sold to local operators while Chevron expands. The weak point is the macro. The economy is contracting, country risk is elevated and Milei is tied in the polls with Axel Kicillof, the Peronist who designed the 2012 seizure of YPF.

## Key Points

- Vaca Muerta supplies about 70% of Argentina's oil. National output hit a record 974,000 barrels a day in September, double the 2017 level.
- The bottleneck is pipelines, not geology. The main oil export line has been full since July 2026.
- Relief arrives in 2027: a new oil pipeline (first cargo in March or April), a larger gas pipeline (by May) and the first of two floating LNG vessels (year-end).
- The next catalyst is November 26, when YPF, Eni and Abu Dhabi's XRG are due to give the final go-ahead to Argentina LNG.
- RIGI gives large projects a 25% tax rate, relief from export duties after three years and thirty years of stability. About US$76bn of projects are approved, nearly all in oil, gas and mining.
- Foreign companies can take cash out again, but only profits earned from 2025 onward.
- Shale crude still pays an 8% export duty whenever Brent is above US$60, the main fiscal irritant left for producers.
- The best wells beat good Permian wells foot for foot but cost about 30% more to drill.
- YPF operates about half the basin's oil. ExxonMobil, Petronas and Equinor have sold to Pluspetrol and Vista, while Chevron has filed a US$13.8bn plan to grow.
- The main risk is political. Milei is tied for 2027 with Axel Kicillof, who designed the 2012 expropriation of YPF.

## Fiscal Regime

**RIGI is the centerpiece, and it is working mostly for oil and gas.** The Incentive Regime for Large Investments, known by its Spanish acronym RIGI, is Milei's flagship investment law. A qualifying project pays corporate tax at 25% instead of 35%, imports equipment free of duty, stops paying export duties after three years and can keep a rising share of its export dollars offshore. Those terms are frozen for thirty years and backed by international arbitration. About US$76bn of projects have been approved, almost all in oil, gas and mining, and the energy list reads like a map of Vaca Muerta: the VMOS oil pipeline, the Southern Energy LNG project, TGS's gas pipeline expansion and upstream developments by Pampa, Pluspetrol and Tecpetrol. The biggest applications are still pending, led by Argentina LNG, a US$25bn YPF oil development and Chevron's El Trapial plan. The window to apply closes in July 2027.

**Outside RIGI, Milei freed prices but kept the tax that hurts most.** His 2024 reform law ended the government's power to set domestic crude prices, so producers now sell at international prices and export freely. The exception is the export duty. Shale crude pays the full 8% whenever Brent is above US$60, while older conventional fields were given relief this year. The Plan Gas fixed-price supply contracts end in 2028 and will not be renewed, so gas producers need export markets to replace them. The provinces own the resource and collect royalties, which in Neuquén are 12% on older licenses and 18% on new ones.

**Foreign companies can get cash out, with one important limit.** Capital controls, known as the cepo, blocked dividends for about ten of the past fifteen years. Since April 2025 the central bank has let companies remit profits earned from fiscal 2025 onward, and they have used the channel heavily: about US$4bn left in the first nine months of 2026, the most in a decade, with energy companies leading. Profits earned before 2025 are still stuck, and holders were offered a dollar bond from the central bank instead. This freedom rests on a central bank rule that a new government could reverse overnight. RIGI projects are better protected, because their currency rights are written into the law itself.

**The macro is the weak leg, and the 2027 election decides it.** Inflation has fallen from triple digits to about 33% a year and the IMF program remains in place. But the economy is contracting, poverty has risen again to 32% and country risk spiked above 900 basis points in early November. Milei's party won the October 2025 midterms yet lacks a majority in either chamber. Polls put him level for 2027 with Axel Kicillof, the Peronist governor of Buenos Aires province, who as deputy economy minister designed the 2012 expropriation of YPF. RIGI is the industry's hedge against that outcome, not a guarantee: Argentina has broken long-term promises to investors before.

## Midstream, Pipelines and LNG

**Today there is one main way out for oil, and it is full.** About three-quarters of the basin's crude travels east on the Oldelval pipeline to the Atlantic port of Puerto Rosales, near Bahía Blanca. Oldelval is owned by the producers themselves, led by YPF and Pluspetrol. An expansion completed in April 2025 more than doubled it to about 540,000 barrels a day, and it was full again by July 2026. A smaller line, OTASA, carries about 110,000 barrels a day over the Andes to a refinery in Chile, and the overflow moves by truck at much higher cost. Argentina's refineries are running near capacity, so almost every additional barrel has to be exported and pipeline space sets the pace of growth.

**VMOS adds three-quarters more capacity, and the first cargo is four months away.** Vaca Muerta Oil Sur is a new 437 km pipeline from the Allen hub to a deep-water terminal at Punta Colorada on the Río Negro coast, the first in Argentina able to load the largest crude tankers. It costs about US$3bn, 70% of it funded by an international bank loan, and is owned by the companies that will ship on it, led by YPF with roughly 30%. Construction is nearly finished. Oil starts filling the line in January 2027, the first cargo is expected in March or April, and capacity builds to 550,000 barrels a day by the end of 2027. A second project, Oldelval's Duplicar Norte, connects the newer northern blocks to the system.

**The projects under way buy the basin room to about 2030.** Oldelval's chief executive says the works in progress lift the basin's capacity from about 720,000 barrels a day today to more than 1.3 million. The industry's goal is 1.5 million barrels a day by 2030. Getting there needs a further round of projects: more pumping on VMOS, which could take it past 700,000 barrels a day, and another line from the northern blocks. BBVA Research puts the infrastructure still needed across oil and gas at US$15-20bn by 2030.

**Gas has the opposite problem: plenty of supply, not enough ways to sell it.** Vaca Muerta can produce far more gas than Argentina uses in summer, yet the country still imports LNG every winter because heating demand spikes and there is no storage. The main artery is the Perito Moreno pipeline, built by the state in 2023 and operated by TGS. TGS is spending its own money to expand it by two-thirds, from 21 to 35 million cubic meters a day (0.7 to 1.2 bcf/d), in time for winter 2027, and the new space was heavily oversubscribed. Gas also flows to Chile, and in small volumes to Brazil through Bolivia.

**LNG is how Argentina plans to turn stranded gas into exports.** Two projects matter. Southern Energy, led by Pan American Energy with YPF, Pampa, Harbour and Golar LNG, has already committed to two floating liquefaction vessels off the Río Negro coast. The first starts up around the end of 2027 and the second in 2028. Together they are modest, about 6 million tons a year, but they are fully sanctioned. The larger prize is Argentina LNG, a YPF-led venture with Italy's Eni and XRG, the international arm of Abu Dhabi's ADNOC. It is twice the size, needs about US$29bn before first LNG in 2031 and lost Shell as a partner in late 2025. YPF says the final go-ahead will be signed on November 26. Financing and gas sales contracts are still open, so the signature starts the test rather than ending it.

## Vaca Muerta: Geology and Operations

**Vaca Muerta sits in the Neuquén Basin of northern Patagonia, about 1,000 km southwest of Buenos Aires.** The formation covers roughly 30,000 square kilometers, about the size of Belgium. Nearly all of today's production comes from Neuquén province, though the formation extends into Mendoza, Río Negro and La Pampa. Activity is concentrated around the town of Añelo, about 100 km northwest of Neuquén city.

**It is one of the largest shale resources on earth, and barely touched.** The US Energy Information Administration put it at 308 trillion cubic feet of recoverable gas and 16 billion barrels of oil, enough to rank Argentina second in the world for shale gas and fourth for shale oil. An Argentine study this year nearly doubled the oil figure, to 30 billion barrels. Only about 5% has been developed. The limit on growth is capital and infrastructure, not drilling inventory.

**The play is divided into windows by what the rock produces.** The shale gets deeper and hotter moving west, so the east produces oil, the west dry gas and the middle a mix rich in condensate. Nearly all the money today goes into the oil window around Añelo. Four blocks there, Loma Campana, La Amarga Chica, Bandurria Sur and Bajada del Palo Oeste, produce just under half the basin's oil. The gas window to the west, anchored by Tecpetrol's Fortín de Piedra, is being drilled less while it waits for pipelines and LNG. A newer "northern hub" of oil blocks, including Chevron's El Trapial, has good rock but higher costs because of distance.

**Vaca Muerta is one formation among several, and it is the one that matters.** It is a marine shale laid down around the end of the Jurassic, the source rock that fed the basin's older conventional fields. It lies about 2,500 to 3,500 meters down (8,000 to 11,500 feet), above the Tordillo sandstone and below the Quintuco limestone. What sets it apart is thickness. The productive section can be several hundred meters thick, far more than most US shales, which lets operators land wells at several levels in the same spot. It is also naturally overpressured, which helps wells flow. Other formations in the basin, notably the deeper Los Molles shale and the Lajas tight gas sands, are known targets but see far less activity.

**Drilling started in 2010 and became an industrial operation over the past decade.** YPF, then controlled by Repsol, drilled the discovery well in 2010 and brought in Chevron in 2013 to develop Loma Campana, still narrowly the largest field. More than 3,300 wells have been drilled since, almost all in Neuquén and almost all horizontal since 2016. Activity is at a record, with 43 rigs running in September.

**A typical well runs two miles sideways and costs US$12-14m.** Operators drill down about 3,000 meters, then sideways for about 3,300 meters (10,800 feet), and fracture the rock in 50 to 60 stages. Wells are drilled in groups from a single pad and stacked at two to five depths, and drilling takes about two weeks. The longest laterals now exceed 5,000 meters. The cost is about 30% above a comparable Permian well, by Chevron's estimate, because rigs, sand and services are scarcer and travel further, and in the northern hub a well can cost US$20m. The payoff is productivity. Rystad puts first-year output at about 30 barrels per lateral foot, against 15 to 23 in the Permian, and Vista says its drilling inventory breaks even below US$45 a barrel.

## The Players

**YPF is the anchor of the basin.** The state-controlled company operates about half of Vaca Muerta's oil, owns the largest stakes in the pipelines and leads Argentina LNG. It is selling its old conventional fields to concentrate on shale. For any other operator, YPF is likely to be a partner, a shipper on the same pipe, or both.

**Behind it sits a group of fast-growing local companies.** Vista Energy is the pure-play shale oil producer and the most active acquirer. Pan American Energy, Pluspetrol, Pampa Energía and Tecpetrol, part of the Techint group that also owns Tenaris, round out the domestic field. These companies have been the buyers as international majors leave.

**The majors have mostly been sellers, with Chevron the clear exception.** ExxonMobil sold to Pluspetrol, and Petronas and Equinor sold to Vista. Shell was reported in January to be sounding out buyers, which its chief executive denied. Chevron is going the other way, with a US$13.8bn, thirty-year plan for El Trapial. TotalEnergies has sold oil acreage to YPF but says it has close to US$10bn still to invest in Argentina, mostly in gas. New US money is appearing too: Continental Resources has a preliminary deal to take half of Phoenix Global Resources, a mid-sized shale oil producer.
