# Argentina Oil & Gas: Vaca Muerta Research Note

As of November 6, 2026. Prepared ahead of the November 16-20, 2026 investor trip to Buenos Aires and Neuquén. Shared editable version: https://claude.ai/artifact/VDYJ6Qp1BV5a5iuT5fw6Fa

## Introduction

Argentina is now the only place outside North America where shale works at scale. Vaca Muerta, a formation in northern Patagonia about the size of Belgium, produces roughly seven of every ten Argentine barrels and has pushed national oil output to a record of nearly one million barrels a day. The rock was never the problem: its wells match or beat the best of the Permian. What held the play back sat above ground, in full pipelines, capital controls that trapped profits and governments that set prices. All three are changing at once. A new oil export pipeline to the Atlantic opens in early 2027, the main gas pipeline grows by two-thirds a month later and Argentina's first LNG exports follow by year-end. President Milei's investment regime, known as RIGI, gives large projects thirty years of tax and currency stability, and foreign companies can once again send dividends home. YPF, the state-controlled champion, runs half the basin, while most global majors have sold to local operators and Chevron is doing the opposite. The open question is political. Milei faces a tight re-election race in 2027, and investors have to judge how much of the reform would survive him.

## Key Points

- Vaca Muerta supplies about 70% of Argentina's oil, and national output is on the cusp of one million barrels a day.
- The bottleneck is pipelines, not geology. The main oil export line has been full since July 2026.
- Relief arrives in 2027: a new oil pipeline (first cargo around March), a bigger gas pipeline (April) and the first floating LNG vessel (year-end).
- The next catalyst is close. YPF, Eni and ADNOC's XRG plan to sanction the Argentina LNG project on November 26, the week after this trip.
- RIGI gives large projects a lower tax rate, duty-free exports and thirty years of stability. Nearly all approved capital is in oil, gas and mining.
- Foreign companies can take cash out again, but only profits earned from 2025 onward. Older earnings remain trapped.
- Shale crude still pays an 8% export duty, the main fiscal irritant left for producers.
- Wells out-produce the Permian but cost about 30% more, because sand, equipment and services are scarcer.
- YPF operates half the basin. ExxonMobil, Petronas and Equinor have sold to local players, while Chevron and TotalEnergies are committing more.
- The main risk is political. Milei is tied in the polls with Peronist governor Axel Kicillof for 2027.

## Fiscal Regime

**RIGI is the centerpiece, and it is working mostly for oil and gas.** The Régimen de Incentivo para Grandes Inversiones is Milei's flagship investment law. A project that qualifies pays corporate tax at 25% instead of 35%, imports equipment free of duty, stops paying export duties after its first few years and can keep a rising share of its export dollars offshore. Those terms are frozen for thirty years, which is the protection investors care about most if the government changes. About US$68bn of projects have been approved, and the list reads like a map of Vaca Muerta: the new VMOS oil pipeline, the Southern Energy LNG project, TGS's gas pipeline expansion and upstream developments by Pampa, Pluspetrol and Tecpetrol. The two largest applications, Argentina LNG and Chevron's El Trapial plan, are still awaiting approval. The window to apply closes in July 2027.

**Outside RIGI, Milei has freed prices but kept one tax that hurts.** His 2024 reform law ended the government's power to set domestic crude prices, and producers can now export freely and sell at international prices. The exception to the liberalizing trend is the export duty. Shale crude still pays 8%, even though older conventional fields were moved this year to a sliding scale tied to the oil price. Gas subsidies under the Plan Gas program expire at the end of 2028, so gas producers will need export markets to replace them. The provinces own the resource and collect royalties, 12% on older licenses and more on new ones.

**Foreign companies can get cash out, with one important limit.** For most of the past two decades Argentina's capital controls, known as the cepo, stopped companies from sending dividends abroad. Since April 2025 the central bank has allowed dividends on profits earned from fiscal 2025 onward. Companies have used the channel heavily, sending about US$4bn abroad in the first nine months of 2026, the most in a decade. Profits earned before 2025 are still stuck, and holders were offered a dollar bond from the central bank instead. Some limits on intercompany loans and on moving between the official and parallel exchange markets also remain. RIGI projects get stronger rights, because their ability to repatriate is tied to the capital they bring in.

**The economy is stable but fragile, and the 2027 election is the swing factor.** Inflation has fallen from triple digits to about 33% a year, the central bank is rebuilding reserves and the IMF program remains in place. But growth has stalled, poverty has ticked back up and country risk moved above 900 basis points in early November. Milei's party won the October 2025 midterms yet lacks a majority in either chamber, so further tax reform is slow. Polls show him level with Axel Kicillof, the Peronist governor of Buenos Aires province, for 2027. RIGI's thirty-year stability clause is the industry's insurance policy against that outcome.

## Midstream, Pipelines and LNG

**Today there is one main way out for oil, and it is full.** About three-quarters of the basin's crude travels east on the Oldelval pipeline to the Atlantic port of Puerto Rosales, near Bahía Blanca. Oldelval is owned by the producers themselves, led by YPF and Pluspetrol, with Chevron and Pan American Energy as the other large holders. It was more than doubled in 2025 and filled up again by July 2026. A smaller line, OTASA, crosses the Andes to a refinery in Chile, and the overflow moves by truck at several times the cost. Because Argentina's refineries are already running flat out, every additional barrel has to be exported, so pipeline space sets the pace of growth.

**VMOS is the project that changes the picture.** Vaca Muerta Oil Sur is a new 437 km pipeline from the basin to a deep-water terminal at Punta Colorada on the Río Negro coast, where the largest crude tankers will be able to load for the first time. It costs about US$3bn, is financed largely by an international bank loan and is owned by the companies that will ship on it. YPF holds roughly 30%, followed by Pluspetrol, Chevron, Pan American, Pampa, Vista, Shell and Tecpetrol. Construction is nearly finished. Oil starts filling the line in January 2027 and the first cargo is expected around March. At full design capacity by the end of 2027 it nearly doubles the basin's pipeline capacity. A second project, Oldelval's Duplicar Norte, connects the newer northern blocks to the system.

**What still needs building is the next leg, not the current one.** With VMOS complete, pipelines should stay ahead of production until around 2028. Reaching the industry's goal of 1.5 million barrels a day by 2030 needs more: extra pumping on VMOS, another line from the northern blocks and more port capacity. The industry puts that bill at over US$5bn. No new refineries are planned.

**Gas has the opposite problem: plenty of supply and not enough demand.** Vaca Muerta can produce far more gas than Argentina uses in summer, yet the country still imports LNG in winter because heating demand spikes and there is no storage. The main gas artery is the Perito Moreno pipeline, opened by the state in 2023 and operated by TGS, which is spending its own money to expand it by two-thirds by April 2027. Demand for the new space was overwhelming, with producers bidding for eleven times what was on offer. Exports to Chile are established. Exports to Brazil are small and have to cross Bolivia.

**LNG is how Argentina plans to turn stranded gas into exports.** Two projects matter. Southern Energy, led by Pan American Energy with YPF, Pampa, Harbour and Golar LNG, has already sanctioned two floating liquefaction vessels that will sit off the Río Negro coast. The first starts up around the end of 2027 and the second in 2028. Together they are modest, about 6 million tons a year, but they make Argentina an LNG exporter. The larger prize is Argentina LNG, a YPF-led venture with Eni and ADNOC's XRG that is twice that size and carries a price tag of roughly US$24bn. Shell walked away from it in late 2025. YPF says the final investment decision will be signed on November 26, with first exports around 2030. Whether it gets financed is the biggest swing factor for long-term gas growth.

## Vaca Muerta: Geology and Operations

**Vaca Muerta sits in the Neuquén Basin of northern Patagonia, about 1,000 km southwest of Buenos Aires.** The formation covers roughly 30,000 square kilometers, about the size of Belgium. Most of it lies in Neuquén province, with extensions into Mendoza, Río Negro and La Pampa. Activity is concentrated around the town of Añelo, about 100 km northwest of Neuquén city.

**It is one of the largest shale resources on earth, and barely touched.** The US Energy Information Administration ranks it second in the world for shale gas and fourth for shale oil, and more recent Argentine estimates roughly double the oil figure. Less than 3% of the resource has been developed. The limit on growth is capital and infrastructure, not drilling inventory.

**The play is divided into windows by what the rock produces.** The shale gets deeper and hotter moving west, so the east produces oil, the west produces dry gas and the middle produces a mix rich in condensate. Nearly all the money today goes into the oil window, in the core blocks around Añelo: Loma Campana, La Amarga Chica, Bandurria Sur and Bajada del Palo Oeste, which together produce most of the basin's oil. The gas window to the west, anchored by Tecpetrol's Fortín de Piedra, is being drilled less while it waits for pipelines and LNG. Operators also talk about a "northern hub" of newer oil blocks, including Chevron's El Trapial, where the rock is good but everything costs more because of distance.

**Vaca Muerta is one formation among several, and it is the one that matters.** It is a marine shale laid down in the late Jurassic, the source rock that fed the basin's older conventional fields. It lies about 2,500 to 3,500 meters down (8,000 to 11,500 feet), on top of the Tordillo sandstone and beneath the Quintuco limestone. What sets it apart is thickness. The oil-rich section can be several hundred meters thick, far thicker than most US shales, which lets operators land wells at several levels in the same spot. It is also naturally overpressured, which helps wells flow. Other formations in the basin, notably the deeper Los Molles shale and the Lajas tight gas sands, are known targets but see far less activity.

**Drilling started in 2010 and became an industrial operation over the past decade.** YPF drilled the discovery well in 2010 and brought in Chevron in 2013 to develop Loma Campana, still the largest field. More than 3,000 wells have been drilled since, almost all in Neuquén province and almost all horizontal since 2016. Activity is at record levels, with more than 40 rigs running.

**A typical well looks like a good Permian well, drilled longer.** Operators drill down about 3,000 meters and then sideways for about 3,300 meters, or two miles, and fracture the rock in 50 to 60 stages. Wells are drilled in groups from a single pad and stacked at two to five different depths, and drilling takes about two weeks. The longest laterals now exceed 5,000 meters. A well costs US$13-14m in the core, about 30% more than a comparable Permian well, mostly because sand, equipment and services are scarcer and travel further. The payoff is productivity. Per foot drilled, these wells out-produce the Permian in their first year, according to Rystad, and the best have each produced more than a million barrels.

## The Players

**YPF is the anchor of the basin.** The state-controlled company operates about half of Vaca Muerta's oil, owns the largest stakes in the pipelines and leads the big LNG project. It is selling its old conventional fields to concentrate on shale. For any other operator in the basin, YPF is likely to be a partner, a shipper on the same pipe, or both.

**Behind it sits a group of fast-growing local companies.** Vista Energy is the pure-play shale oil producer and the most active acquirer. Pan American Energy, Pluspetrol, Pampa Energía and Tecpetrol, part of the Techint group that also owns Tenaris, round out the domestic field. These companies have been the buyers as international majors leave.

**The majors have mostly been sellers, with two exceptions.** ExxonMobil, Petronas and Equinor have all sold their positions in the past two years, and Shell has been reported to be testing buyer interest, which it denies. Chevron is going the other way, with a US$13.8bn long-term plan for its El Trapial block, and TotalEnergies has pledged new investment in its gas assets. New US money is starting to arrive too. Continental Resources agreed this year to buy half of Phoenix Global Resources, a mid-sized shale oil producer.
